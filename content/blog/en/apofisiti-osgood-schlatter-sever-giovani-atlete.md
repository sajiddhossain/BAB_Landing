There is a sentence that accompanies almost every young athlete's growth: "it's just growing pains, they'll pass." It usually arrives when a twelve- or thirteen-year-old says her knee hurts just below the kneecap, or that her heel burns through the first ten minutes of the warm-up and then "warms up."

Those two pains have precise names — **Osgood-Schlatter disease** and **Sever's disease** — and one thing in common: they are not tears, they do not come from a single trauma, and they are not in the muscle. They sit at the point where a tendon pulls on a piece of bone that has not finished forming.

The other thing they share is less reassuring: they do not always resolve on their own, and in the meantime they eat months of training. This article sets out what they are, how long they really last in the data, why girls are not outside the picture, and what can be done instead of waiting.

> **In brief**
> - Osgood-Schlatter, Sever's and Sinding-Larsen-Johansson are **traction apophysitis** and account for **more than 99%** of lower-limb apophysitis in children and adolescents (Wedderkopp et al., 2026).
> - Median duration **3-4 weeks**, real range **1 to 45 weeks**, in a prospective cohort of **878 girls and 792 boys** followed for 5.5 years (Wedderkopp et al., 2026).
> - In athletes who reach a clinic the bill is steeper: **3.2 months** of complete stoppage and **7.3 months** of training that was not fully effective (Kujala et al., 1985).
> - "It resolves on its own" is not a reliable forecast: at a median of **3.75 years** after diagnosis, **60.5%** of 43 patients still had Osgood-Schlatter pain (Guldhammer et al., 2019).
> - It is not a boys-only matter: the classic peak in boys aged 12-15 **is no longer found as a clear sex difference**, because of greater female participation in high-impact sports (Ladenhauf et al., 2020).
> - Higher risk in **football, handball, basketball and jumping gymnastics** (risk ratios **2.07-2.74**); extra physical education hours did **not** increase risk (Wedderkopp et al., 2026).
> - Against passive rest: **80%** successful outcomes at 12 weeks and **90%** at 12 months with progressive loading and strengthening, in 51 adolescents aged 10-14 (51% girls) who arrived with 21 months of pain (Rathleff et al., 2020).
> - Insoles, heel raises and taping: evidence of **low to very low certainty**, in samples that were **73% male** (Cochrane review; Williams et al., 2026).

## What is traction apophysitis?

**It is a tendon's attachment point becoming irritated because, in a growing body, it is the weakest link in the chain.** An **apophysis** is a bone growth centre where a tendon inserts: until it fuses it stays cartilaginous, and it is less resistant than the tendon pulling on it. This is the same reason an explosive sprint in an adolescent athlete can cause a [pelvic apophyseal avulsion](/en/blog/dolore-inguine-giovani-atlete) rather than a muscle strain.

In the lower limb there are three names worth knowing, and they cover almost every case seen:

- **Osgood-Schlatter disease** — the tibial tuberosity, the bony bump just below the kneecap where the patellar tendon inserts. It hurts in one spot, often swollen, and flares with jumping, running, stairs and kneeling.
- **Sever's disease** (calcaneal apophysitis) — the heel bone's apophysis, where the Achilles tendon pulls. It hurts at the heel, typically at the start of activity and on hard surfaces, and is reproduced by squeezing the heel from both sides.
- **Sinding-Larsen-Johansson syndrome** — the lower pole of the kneecap, the other end of the same patellar tendon.

In the Danish prospective cohort that followed **878 girls and 792 boys** for 5.5 years, with parents reporting injuries by weekly text message followed by clinical assessment, these three together made up **more than 99%** of lower-limb apophysitis ([Wedderkopp et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02328-w)).

## How long does it really last?

**From one week to almost a year: the median is 3-4 weeks, but the range runs from 1 to 45 weeks.** This is the most useful figure in the Danish study, because it comes from a general population of children rather than from those who have already ended up in an orthopaedic clinic ([Wedderkopp et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02328-w)).

That range is the whole difference between two conversations. A month of manageable discomfort with reduced load is a parenthesis. Ten months is a season: it changes the relationship with the team, with one's own body, and with the idea of being "the one who always gets injured."

In sporting populations the bill gets worse. In the historical Finnish study of **68 young athletes** with Osgood-Schlatter followed at a sports medicine clinic, pain had **completely stopped training for an average of 3.2 months** and interfered with fully effective training for **7.3 months**; mean onset was at **13.1 years** ([Kujala et al., 1985](https://doi.org/10.1177/036354658501300404)). It is a retrospective questionnaire from the 1980s, with all the attendant limits — the management prescribed then averaged two months of rest from any painful activity — but the age figure remains instructive: it falls squarely inside the [growth spurt](/en/blog/picco-di-crescita-giovani-atlete).

## "It passes with growth": is that true?

**Often yes, but not often enough to count on it.** Recent literature has corrected exactly this point. In a retrospective cohort of patients diagnosed with Osgood-Schlatter between 2010 and 2016 and contacted again in 2018, **60.5% of the 43 respondents still reported knee pain** related to Osgood-Schlatter at a median of **3.75 years** after diagnosis; among them, **42.9%** had daily pain and **54%** had reduced their sport, with lower function and quality-of-life scores ([Guldhammer et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/2325967119878136)).

It should be read for what it is: **43 patients out of 84 contacted**, retrospective, single-centre, with the risk that those still in pain answer more readily. It does not say "60% do not recover." It says "it will pass with growth" is not a forecast to build two years of waiting on, and that some adolescents with knee pain carry it into adulthood — the same pattern seen in [patellofemoral pain](/en/blog/dolore-ginocchio-femoro-rotuleo-giovani-atlete), where 40.5% still have pain at five years.

## Is it only a boys' problem?

**No, and the gap between the sexes has narrowed as girls have taken up the same sports, at the same volumes.** The 2020 review in *Current Opinion in Pediatrics* states it explicitly: the documented peak in boys aged **12-15** is no longer found as a clear difference in sex distribution, and the authors attribute this to **greater female participation in high-impact sports** ([Ladenhauf et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1097/MOP.0000000000000842)). It is a narrative review, not a measurement: it counts as clinical orientation, not as a statistic.

Imaging data tell a more conservative story. In a population study of **1,910 Dutch adolescents aged 12-15 (52% girls)** who underwent MRI of **both knees** regardless of symptoms, Osgood-Schlatter features appeared in **2.3%** and Sinding-Larsen-Johansson features in **0.8%**; Osgood-Schlatter was more frequent in boys (**OR 4.21**; 95% CI 2.01-8.85) ([Kemmeren et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465241277162)). The authors also warn about the flip side: nearly one adolescent in five had **at least one abnormal finding** on MRI, and knowing these findings are common is what prevents over-treating those without symptoms.

The two things sit together: in imaging, boys remain over-represented; in clinical practice, girls are no longer the exception. For anyone coaching a girls' team the consequence is simple — **a thirteen-year-old's knee or heel pain should not be filed away as "a boys' thing."**

## Which loads and which sports raise the risk?

**Those that repeat jumps, sprints and changes of direction — not physical activity in general.** In the Danish cohort the incidence was between **3.2 and 7.0 cases per 1,000 participations** in leisure-time sport, and risk was higher in **football, handball, basketball and jumping gymnastics**, with risk ratios between **2.07 and 2.74** ([Wedderkopp et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02328-w)).

The same study contains the result worth remembering before taking movement away from a young girl: **extra hours of physical education at school did not increase the risk of apophysitis.** The problem is not moving, it is repeating the same movement on the same tendon without variation and without recovery — one of the documented costs of [early specialisation](/en/blog/specializzazione-precoce-giovani-atlete).

The moment when all of this concentrates is predictable: the years around the growth spurt, when bone lengthens before muscles and tendons adapt, and **load management** becomes the variable that can actually be changed.

## Rest or load? What the evidence says

**Complete rest is no longer the reference, at least for Osgood-Schlatter.** In the prospective cohort of **51 adolescents aged 10-14 (51% girls)**, who arrived with a mean pain duration of **21 months**, a 12-week pathway of **graded load progression** on the patellar tendon, pain monitoring, strengthening exercises and gradual return to sport produced **80% self-reported successful outcomes at 12 weeks** and **90% at 12 months**; the share back playing went from **16% at 12 weeks to 69% at one year**, with gains in knee extension strength (+32%) and hip abduction strength (+24%) ([Rathleff et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/2325967120911106)).

Two honest caveats. First: the study had **no control group**, so it does not prove that pathway beats waiting — it shows it is feasible and leads to good outcomes in girls and boys who had been in pain for nearly two years. Second: return is **not immediate**. At three months a minority were playing; most of the return came later.

On what aggravates the pain, a cross-sectional study of **35 adolescents with Osgood-Schlatter (48.5% female, mean age 13.0)** and 21 controls measured pain across eight different movements: the most selective was the **single-leg squat**, with a mean difference of **4.2 points out of 10** against controls, and more generally single-leg movements that load the tuberosity through the tendon ([Holden et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.14729)). That is practical information: you know where to start subtracting, and where to return as pain settles.

And the products? The **2026 Cochrane review** of non-surgical treatments for lower-limb apophyseal injuries included **10 randomised trials and 654 children** (mean ages 10.3-13.3, **73% male**): drugs, taping, foot orthoses, heel lifts and heel cushioning. The evidence is rated mostly **low or very low certainty**; custom orthoses and heel lifts are essentially equivalent (moderate certainty); **no trial measured quality of life**, even though cohort studies show these conditions damage it ([Williams et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD015156.pub2)). For Sever's disease alone, a systematic review of **8 randomised trials** concludes that conservative treatment — insoles, exercise, kinesio taping — relieves symptoms, with fair average methodological quality (PEDro 6.75) ([Hernandez-Lucas et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm13051391)).

The summary a family can use: **no object cures apophysitis**, and money spent on insoles does not replace the conversation about how many sessions, how many jumps, and with what progression.

## Are there complications to know about?

**Few and rare, but two deserve a line.** The first is tibial tubercle fracture: in an analysis of a large US insurance database with **146,672 patients** with Osgood-Schlatter, the one-year rate was **0.62%**, with an incidence of **627.3 cases per 100,000 person-years** against **42.7** in controls; male sex and obesity were associated with higher risk ([Milner et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1097/BPO.0000000000002757)). It is a high relative risk on an event that remains rare, and it comes from administrative data — billing codes, not consultations.

The second comes from **341 elite academy footballers, all male**: those with a history of Osgood-Schlatter were far more likely to sustain a **pars interarticularis fracture** (relative risk **19.27**) ([Blanchard et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2026.01.020)). It is a retrospective exploratory study in an **exclusively male** sample: it does not transfer to girls. It does serve as a reminder not to file away [back pain](/en/blog/mal-di-schiena-giovani-atlete) too quickly in an athlete who has had apophysitis and keeps loading heavily.

## What can a club do, starting Monday?

Four concrete things, none of them clinical.

**Ask where it hurts, with one finger.** "Point to the spot" separates pinpoint bone pain from diffuse pain around the kneecap in ten seconds. It is not a diagnosis, it is the filter that tells you whether a medical review is worth it.

**Reduce, don't zero out.** Taking everything away for a month is the fastest way to lose an athlete: the load-progression ladder exists precisely to keep a girl in pain involved, with different movements and lower volumes ([Rathleff et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/2325967120911106)). Jumps and single-leg squats are the first candidates to remove; cycling, strength work at controlled pain and play at reduced intensity are the first to keep.

**Don't reward gritting teeth.** [Normalised pain](/en/blog/dolore-in-puberta-neuroscienza) is why apophysitis reaches a doctor after months. A [coach's words](/en/blog/parole-allenatore-salute-atlete) decide whether a girl reports pain on day three or in month three.

**Look at the calendar before the symptom.** Two teams, a weekend tournament, the return after the summer break at June's volume: that is where apophysitis flares, in the years when bone outruns tendon.

## BAB's role

BAB does not diagnose: a knee that hurts below the kneecap or a heel that has burned for weeks is assessed in a consultation, not in an app.

What it offers is a **private athlete space** where a girl records load, pain, energy, sleep and her cycle over time. For apophysitis this does two very concrete things: it lets her bring a precise history to the doctor — since when, after which sessions, with what trend — instead of "my knee hurts sometimes"; and it shows, week by week, whether the load progression is working or merely moving the problem. Clubs receive **only aggregated, anonymous data**, never an individual athlete's diary.

The educational half is this article: giving a name to a pain that gets dismissed as inevitable, and saying that between "it'll pass" and "stop" there is a documented third road.

## When to see a professional

**When pain lasts more than two to three weeks despite reduced load, or when it changes character.** The first port of call is the paediatrician or family doctor, who assesses and refers if needed.

See someone without waiting for: pain **present at rest or at night**; **significant swelling, fever, redness, warmth** or a limp that does not settle; pain that began with a **single trauma** and leaves the athlete unable to load — when in doubt, a tuberosity fracture must be ruled out by a doctor, not by a coach; and any situation where the athlete **is quietly giving things up** to avoid the pain. For non-traumatic knee pain there is also a shared decision-making support tool developed together with adolescents and doctors, designed to explain the diagnosis and put the options on the table ([Riel et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.msksp.2025.103293)).
