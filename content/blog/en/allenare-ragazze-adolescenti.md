faq:
  - q: "How many teenage athletes does a club actually lose?"
    a: "Far more than it records. Tracking the actual registrations of 29,225 female athletes over seven years, among those who joined aged 10-14 only 13% participated continuously and 71% dropped out without ever returning (Eime et al., 2020). In the UK, 43% of girls aged 11-18 say they loved sport as children and have left it, against 24% of boys (Women in Sport, 2022 — not peer-reviewed)."
  - q: "Does a coach need to become an expert on the menstrual cycle and RED-S?"
    a: "No, and a coach should neither diagnose nor know each athlete's individual data. What's needed is baseline literacy, and that is measurable: in the Female Athlete Health Report 2023 (769 athletes, UK), those who perceived their coach as «very well-informed» reported on average 3.2 RED-S symptoms against 4.3 among those who perceived their coach as uninformed — 36% fewer. Note that the report's mean age is 32, so it describes the adult athletic population: it points a direction, not a prevalence in adolescents."
  - q: "Why shouldn't I look at individual athletes' health data?"
    a: "Because privacy is what makes trust possible. The moment an athlete fears her cycle or her mood could end up on display in front of the group, she stops telling the truth — and the data you get becomes useless as well as intrusive. Aggregated, anonymous signals let you schedule a deload or change the tone of a heavy week without asking anyone to expose herself."
  - q: "What are the concrete levers for coaching teenage girls well?"
    a: "There are four, and none of them needs a budget. 1) Talk about the changing body in plain, non-judgemental language. 2) Adjust the load instead of penalising the dip — starting with a 20-minute neuromuscular warm-up, twice a week. 3) Use aggregated, anonymous team signals, never the individual health data point. 4) Guarantee changing spaces with privacy, menstrual products on site, and continuity in the adults around the team. The first two move measurable health outcomes; the other two decide whether an athlete trusts the place enough to stay."
  - q: "How do you adjust the load for an athlete who is growing fast?"
    a: "By holding it steady rather than increasing it during the quarter she grows. The physiological reason is that peak bone mineral accrual arrives roughly six months after peak height velocity (Bailey et al., 1999): for a period, bone is already longer but not yet as dense. A fixed wall measure and one measurement every three months is enough to spot who is growing. It should be said honestly that in girls the evidence directly linking maturation to injury is still limited (Zoellner and Whatman, 2026): this is a reason for caution, not an alarm."
  - q: "Should an athlete with period pain skip training?"
    a: "Not by default, and the decision is hers either way. The available evidence places exercise among the studied treatments for menstrual pain, not among its risk factors: a Cochrane review of 12 studies and 854 women estimates a large reduction in pain intensity compared with doing nothing (SMD -1.86; 95% CI -2.06 to -1.66), with the quality of evidence rated low (Armour et al., 2019). The correct message is therefore neither \"rest until it passes\" nor \"grit your teeth\", but \"moving, if you feel up to it, is probably part of the solution\". Staff need nothing more than three practical conditions: toilet access, products available, and the option to adapt the session without having to justify it."
  - q: "How common is period pain among athletes?"
    a: "It is the most frequent menstrual cycle disorder in this population: a systematic review of 60 studies and 6,380 athletes estimates a dysmenorrhoea prevalence of 32.3%, with a very wide range across studies — from 7.8% to 85.6% — driven by different definitions and measurement methods (Taim et al., 2023). For a coach the more useful number is another one: between 25.2% and 61.1% of adolescents aged 10-18 avoid or reduce physical activity during menstruation (Harvey et al., 2025). Those are lost sessions no injury register counts."
  - q: "When should period pain be flagged to the family?"
    a: "When it stops being an episode and becomes a pattern: pain that costs training sessions or school days every month, or that does not improve within 3-6 months of starting treatment, is the point at which the clinical reference written for adolescents says secondary causes should be investigated (ACOG, 2018). A coach does not diagnose and does not ask for details: they notice that the pattern exists and tell the family, who will decide whether to take it to a healthcare professional."
  - q: "How do you prevent serious injuries in teenage female athletes?"
    a: "Through the warm-up, done in a specific way and consistently. A meta-analysis of 27,231 female athletes estimated that preventive neuromuscular training reduces ACL injury risk from about 1 in 54 to 1 in 111 (OR 0.51; 95% CI 0.37-0.69), and the effect is strongest in the 13-19 band (OR 0.38; 95% CI 0.24-0.60), roughly 60% less risk (Petushek et al., 2019). The average dose across effective programmes is about 24 minutes per session, 2.5 times a week. The decisive variable isn't which exercises you pick, but consistency: below ~66% overall compliance the benefit does not appear (Sugimoto et al., 2012)."

Among girls who join a sports club aged **10-14, 71% drop out without ever returning** (Eime et al., 2020). For a club that's not just a social statistic: it's talent walking out the door and a squad thinning out in exactly the years it should be growing. The good news? Much of what keeps an athlete in the game runs through the hands of the person coaching her.

> **Key points**
> - Among those who join aged **10-14**, only **13%** participate continuously over seven years; **71%** drop out without returning (Eime et al., 2020).
> - A coach perceived as **very well-informed** is associated with **36% fewer** RED-S symptoms in his or her athletes (Female Athlete Health Report, 2023 — adult sample).
> - Between **25%** and **61%** of adolescent girls avoid or reduce physical activity during their period (Harvey et al., 2025).
> - The four levers: **talk about the body**, **adjust the load**, **use anonymous team signals**, **create space and continuity**.

## Why does a teenage athlete stop turning up to training?

Almost never because she has lost interest: far more often because **the context has stopped working for her**. It's easy to read a drop in attendance as "she's lost interest." It almost never is. In most cases the athlete feels **out of place** precisely as her body changes: periods, swinging energy, a growing chest, eyes that weigh on her. If the environment doesn't talk about it, she's left alone with questions she doesn't dare ask — and she leaves in silence.

This isn't an impression. A longitudinal study of 781 Canadian adolescents found that **girls who started puberty earlier were significantly more likely to discontinue organised activities** (HR 1.68) than peers with average pubertal timing (Gallant et al., 2023). And between **25% and 61%** of adolescent girls avoid or reduce physical activity during their period — for pain, but also for fear of leaking and embarrassment (Harvey et al., 2025). Coaching teenage girls well means, first of all, building a context where staying is easier than leaving. [Female drop-out](/blog/abbandono-puberta) isn't fate: it's an outcome.

## 1. Talk about the changing body (without embarrassment)

You don't need to be a doctor. You need to **normalise** it: name the cycle, the tiredness, the body changes in plain, non-judgemental language. When the coach shows it's a topic you can talk about, the athlete stops hiding it — and stops hiding herself.

There's a number here too. In the Female Athlete Health Report 2023, athletes who perceived their coach as **very well-informed** reported on average **3.2 RED-S symptoms against 4.3** among those who perceived their coach as uninformed: **36% fewer**. The report's mean age is 32 — it describes the adult athletic population, not thirteen-year-olds — but the direction is clear, and we dig into it in [a coach's words](/blog/parole-allenatore-salute-atlete).

## 2. Adjust the load, don't judge the dip

An adolescent athlete's energy isn't constant, and that's fine. An "amber" day isn't laziness: it's a body working on many fronts at once. **Adjusting the load** — instead of always demanding the maximum or penalising the dip — is what keeps a girl on the field longer. The message that matters: *listening to yourself isn't weakness, it's strategy.*

And there is one load adjustment worth more than all the others, because it has been measured: **twenty minutes of neuromuscular warm-up, twice a week**, cuts ACL injury risk by roughly **60%** in athletes aged 13-19 (Petushek et al., 2019). It isn't an addition to the programme: it takes the place of the warm-up already being done, and [it only works if it is actually done](/blog/crociato-giovani-atlete) — below ~66% compliance the benefit disappears (Sugimoto et al., 2012).

There is one moment when adjusting the load matters more than any other, and you spot it with a wall measure: the quarter in which an athlete grows fast. Peak bone mineral accrual comes **roughly six months after** peak height velocity (Bailey et al., 1999): for a period, bone is already longer but not yet as dense. That doesn't mean stopping — it means holding volume steady and working on technique and control, as we explain in [the growth spurt in young female athletes](/blog/picco-di-crescita-giovani-atlete).

Then there is the load adjustment decided in thirty seconds rather than over a week: what happens after a blow to the head. Across sex-comparable sports, **female athletes are 1.26 times more likely not to be removed from activity** after a concussion (95% CI 1.09-1.45) (Zynda et al., 2021) — and those who stay on the field recover in 44 days instead of 22 (Elbin et al., 2016). There is one rule, and [it is worth writing it down before you need it](/blog/commozione-cerebrale-giovani-atlete): if a concussion is suspected, the athlete comes off and does not return that day.

## 3. Use team signals, never individual data

Understanding how the team is doing lets you act before overload — schedule a deload, change the tone of a heavy week. But this must be done with **aggregated, anonymous signals**: never expose an individual athlete's data. Privacy isn't red tape, it's the condition for girls to trust you and open up. The moment an athlete fears her cycle or her mood could end up on display in front of everyone, she stops telling you the truth.

## 4. Create space and continuity

Small things make a big difference: **privacy** in changing rooms, period products available, continuity in the people they look up to. A girl who feels seen and safe doesn't need to "disappear" when her body tests her.

And watch what looks like a kit detail: among girls aged 11-18, **46% say their breasts affect their participation in compulsory sport** — rising to **51% at exactly 13-14** — yet only **10%** always wear a sports bra (Scurr et al., 2016). It's a problem with [a simple fix](/blog/reggiseno-sportivo-ragazze), one that costs nothing and that nobody names.

## Should an athlete with period pain skip training?

**Not by default — and the decision stays with her.** The available evidence places exercise among the studied *treatments* for menstrual pain, not among its risk factors: a Cochrane review of 12 studies and 854 women estimates a large reduction in pain intensity compared with doing nothing (SMD -1.86; 95% CI -2.06 to -1.66), with the quality of evidence rated **low** (Armour et al., 2019). Translated for a coach: the correct message is neither "rest until it passes" nor "grit your teeth", but "moving, if you feel up to it, is probably part of the solution".

This is not a hypothetical. Among athletes, dysmenorrhoea is the **most frequent** menstrual cycle disorder: 32.3% across 60 studies and 6,380 athletes, with a huge range between studies (7.8-85.6%) that speaks to the uncertainty, not to rarity (Taim et al., 2023). And between **25.2% and 61.1%** of adolescents avoid or reduce physical activity during menstruation (Harvey et al., 2025): lost sessions that no injury register counts.

Staff need nothing beyond three practical conditions — toilet access, menstrual products available, the option to adapt the session without justifying it in front of the group — and **one threshold to know**: if pain does not improve within **3-6 months** of starting treatment, the clinical reference written for adolescents says secondary causes should be investigated (ACOG, 2018). That is not a coach's job to do: it is a coach's job to notice the pattern and tell the family. The full picture, with what actually works, is in [period pain and sport](/blog/dolori-mestruali-giovani-atlete).

## Why does retaining athletes pay off for the club too?

Because every girl who stays is a squad that doesn't thin out. Retaining athletes isn't only ethics: it's **sporting sustainability**. Every girl who stays is an investment that keeps returning value to the team — fewer dropouts, a more solid squad, steadier results over time. Whoever builds an environment today where teenage girls stay will have the strongest teams a few years from now.
