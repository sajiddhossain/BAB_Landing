In 2023 Italy published its **first national survey** attempting to measure how widespread violence is inside youth sport. The most quoted result is a large number: **almost four in ten** people who played organised sport as minors report having experienced at least one form of interpersonal violence inside that setting.

It is a number to handle carefully — we will look at how it was produced and why it is not a population estimate — but the part that concerns clubs does not hinge on the second decimal place. It hinges on three findings the data show consistently: the most frequent form of violence is not the kind that makes headlines, it happens in the ordinary places of sport, and hardly anyone talks about it while it is happening.

Meanwhile, since 2021 Italy has had a legal framework: every amateur sports club must have an organisational model, a code of conduct and a named person responsible. All of it is verifiable by anyone — including a parent who suspects nobody.

> **In short**
> - First Italian survey, **1,446 adults aged 18-30** who played organised sport as minors: **38.6%** report at least one form of interpersonal violence experienced inside sport before age 18.
> - By category: **psychological 30.4%**, **physical 18.6%**, **neglect 14.5%**, non-contact sexual **10.3%**, contact sexual **9.6%**; **19.4%** report more than one form.
> - Who: **teammates 33.1%**, **coaches 31.1%**. Among **women**, coaches rise to **35.0%**.
> - Where: **62.5%** inside or around the facility, **25.8%** in changing rooms and showers.
> - **The majority neither asked for nor received help**; among women, **62.3%**. The most common reason: **46.5%** considered the behaviours **acceptable or tolerable**.
> - Consequences: **32.3%** left sport — **37.4%** among women.
> - **Stated limit**: online convenience sample, retrospective data, **report not peer-reviewed**. It indicates an order of magnitude, not a national prevalence.
> - **In Italy**, since 31 December 2024 every amateur club must have a **safeguarding officer**, an **organisational model** and a **code of conduct** (Legislative Decree 36/2021, art. 33(6); Legislative Decree 39/2021, art. 16).

## What do we mean by violence in sport?

Not one single thing, and that is the first reason the numbers surprise people.

International research uses the term **interpersonal violence** and divides it into five categories: **psychological violence**, **physical violence**, **neglect**, **non-contact sexual violence** and **contact sexual violence**. The most recent International Olympic Committee consensus, built on a review that retrieved more than twenty-four thousand citations in nine languages, adopts this structure and insists on one point: violence in sport is almost always relational and ongoing, not an isolated event ([Tuakli-Wosornu et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2024-108766)).

The methodological consequence matters. The questionnaires do not ask "have you experienced violence?": they list **concrete situations** and ask whether they happened. Examples used in the Italian survey include being asked, ordered or forced to do physical exercise as a punishment (physical violence), being criticised for one's physical appearance (psychological violence), and being supervised so inadequately during sport as to be put at risk (neglect) ([ChangeTheGame, 2023](https://www.sport.governo.it/media/tnyji12g/indagine-violenza-sport-change-the-game-02-11-23.pdf)).

So 38.6% does not mean four athletes in ten were victims of a crime. It means four in ten report at least one experience falling into these categories — many of which, inside sport, have long been considered normal. That is exactly what the instrument is designed to surface, and it should be said every time the number is quoted.

## How widespread is it in Italy?

The sample is **1,446 adults aged 18-30** who before the age of 18 had played organised sport in Italy — that is, in a club, league or team with organised training and competition, excluding school physical education. 50.8% are women. Data were collected online by Nielsen from a panel, using a questionnaire adapted from the European CASES project.

The results: **38.6%** (558 people) report having experienced at least one form of interpersonal violence inside sport before turning 18. By category: **psychological violence 30.4%**, **physical violence 18.6%**, **neglect 14.5%**, **non-contact sexual violence 10.3%**, **contact sexual violence 9.6%**. **19.4%** report more than one form ([ChangeTheGame, 2023](https://www.sport.governo.it/media/tnyji12g/indagine-violenza-sport-change-the-game-02-11-23.pdf)).

On age of onset, the figure that speaks most directly to youth categories: for **psychological** violence **39%** and for **physical** violence **28%** of participants say the behaviours began **before age 14**. For sexual violence and neglect the concentration is higher between 15 and 16.

The limits belong with the numbers, and the authors state them: this is a **convenience sample** recruited from an internet panel, not a probability sample of the Italian population; the data are **retrospective**, collected years after the events; and the document is a **project report, not a peer-reviewed article**. People who take part in a survey on this topic may also be more invested than average. The number indicates an order of magnitude and a direction — it is not the national prevalence.

## Do the European numbers say the same?

They say something worse, with a similar method but published in a journal.

The **CASES** study applied the same design to **10,302 adults aged 18-30** in six European countries — Austria, Belgium, Germany, Romania, Spain and the United Kingdom, **not Italy** — who had played organised sport as minors. Prevalence inside sport: **psychological violence 65%**, **physical 44%**, **neglect 37%**, **non-contact sexual 35%**, **contact sexual 20%**, with broadly similar patterns across countries ([Hartill et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2023.106513)).

The European figures are much higher than the Italian ones. That is not a story about Italy: two surveys with convenience samples, differently translated wording and different countries cannot be compared one to one. The honest reading is that both place the phenomenon at a scale no sports club can treat as marginal.

## Are girls more at risk than boys?

Here the data contradict the expectation, and we say so in full, because that is how you test whether a source is trustworthy.

In the Italian sample **men report higher prevalence** of psychological, physical and sexual violence; women report more **only for neglect** ([ChangeTheGame, 2023](https://www.sport.governo.it/media/tnyji12g/indagine-violenza-sport-change-the-game-02-11-23.pdf)). In the European study too, overall prevalence inside sport was higher in males ([Hartill et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2023.106513)). Anyone claiming that "girls' sport is more dangerous" is saying something these data do not support.

Three differences do concern girls specifically, and they are the ones a club can act on:

- **Who**: women name the **coach** as responsible for the most severe experience in **35.0%** of cases, against 27.0% for men; men more often name teammates (36.8% versus 27.8%). The relationship inside which the harm occurs is different, so the measure that helps is different too.
- **Silence**: **62.3%** of women neither asked for nor received help, against a 54-58% average across the two age bands.
- **Leaving**: **37.4%** of women who experienced violence **left sport** (men 27.0%) and **32.6%** changed discipline (men 27.4%).

That last point connects to a theme covered elsewhere on this blog: [dropping out of sport during puberty](/en/blog/abbandono-puberta) is almost always explained by school, a changing body and boredom. These data suggest that in some cases the reason is a different one, and it appears in no motivation questionnaire.

## Who is on the other side, and where does it happen?

In the most severe experiences reported, the person causing harm is almost always **inside the group**: **teammates the athlete knew in 33.1%** of cases, **coaches in 31.1%**, other sport staff in 14.7%, unknown adults in 8.4%, other known adults in 8.1% ([ChangeTheGame, 2023](https://www.sport.governo.it/media/tnyji12g/indagine-violenza-sport-change-the-game-02-11-23.pdf)).

The report itself warns that the word "perpetrator" is imprecise for part of these cases: many of the situations captured are **behaviours normalised among peers or tolerated by adults** — insults about appearance, sexist or homophobic language let slide, group initiations — rather than criminal conduct. The distinction exists to make intervention possible, not to minimise: adults who do not intervene are part of the mechanism.

On places: **62.5%** inside or immediately around the facility, **25.8%** in **changing rooms and showers**, 16.7% in public settings, then private homes (7.7%), medical treatment rooms (7.3%) and cars or vehicles (7.2%). One respondent could name several places, so the percentages overlap.

From which follows the least romantic and most useful point in this article: **the measures that matter are rules about ordinary spaces** — who enters the changing rooms, how away trips are run, who drives whom, where treatments happen — not awareness campaigns about outside dangers.

## Why does hardly anyone speak up?

Because while it is happening, in most cases, it does not feel like something to speak up about.

The majority of the sample — **54.5%** of 18-24 year olds and **57.7%** of 25-30 year olds, **62.3% of women** — neither asked for nor received help. Among those who did not seek it, the stated reasons are, in order: **46.5% thought the behaviours were acceptable or tolerable**, **30.1% did not want to appear weak**, **25.3% did not know who to turn to**, **17% feared the consequences**, and **3.2%** had been **told not to talk about it** ([ChangeTheGame, 2023](https://www.sport.governo.it/media/tnyji12g/indagine-violenza-sport-change-the-game-02-11-23.pdf)).

The first reason is the most instructive and the hardest to correct: almost half of those who experienced something considered it **tolerable**. That is the product of a culture in which shouting, humiliating someone in front of the group, commenting on bodies and punishing with exercise were for decades synonyms for taking sport seriously. The same dynamic that makes it hard to [talk about periods with a coach](/en/blog/parlare-di-ciclo-con-allenatore) — "you don't discuss that", "it's my business" — makes it hard to say that a sentence hurt.

When help does arrive, it mostly arrives from outside: **family 46.9%**, **friends 31.8%**, **someone in the sport setting 25.5%**, mental health professionals 10.9%, legal help 6%. In these numbers, one trusted adult outside the sport context is worth more than any internal procedure.

## What remains, years later?

Two kinds of consequence, measured in different populations and with different designs.

The first is **leaving sport**. In the Italian sample, among those reporting violence, **32.3% left sport**, 29.9% changed discipline and 22.2% changed club; 12.9% report temporary health problems and 6.5% chronic ones, while 20.4% report no consequences. Among women, leaving rises to **37.4%**.

The second is **health over time**. In a study of adults who had played sport as minors in Belgium and the Netherlands, those reporting **severe** violence experienced in sport as children reported **more mental health problems and lower quality of life** as adults than those who had not ([Vertommen et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2017.12.013)). In the same sample, 9% reported severe psychological violence, 8% severe physical violence and 6% severe sexual violence.

The limit is the same for both: these are **retrospective, observational studies in adults**, showing associations rather than causes, and concerning adult populations in northern Europe, not thirteen-year-old Italian athletes. The direction, however, is consistent across the available literature.

## What must an Italian sports club have by law?

This is the verifiable part, and it requires suspecting no one.

Italy's sport reform introduced specific obligations for amateur sports clubs. In short:

- an **organisational and control model** for sporting activity, setting out procedures, roles and reporting channels;
- a **code of conduct** protecting minors and prohibiting harassment, violence and discrimination;
- the appointment of a **Responsabile contro abusi, violenze e discriminazioni** — a *safeguarding officer* — whose name must be **published on the club's website** and communicated to its federation.

The legal basis is **article 33(6) of Legislative Decree 36/2021** and **article 16 of Legislative Decree 39/2021**, implemented through guidelines adopted by the federations and sport promotion bodies. The deadline for appointing the officer in affiliated clubs was **31 December 2024**; failing to do so has consequences for federation affiliation.

Translated for a family: **the officer's name, the code of conduct and the organisational model are documents you are owed**. Asking for them is not an accusation — it is like asking where the fire exit is.

## How can you tell whether a club takes it seriously?

Not from the atmosphere, which is almost always good, and not from the people, who are almost always decent. You can tell from how a club answers six questions.

1. **Who is the safeguarding officer, and where is that written?** Name, contact, page on the website.
2. **Is there a written code of conduct, and have the athletes read it?** A code known only to the board does not do its job.
3. **What is the rule about changing rooms?** Who may enter, when, and what happens when an adult is needed inside.
4. **How are away trips and overnight stays handled?** Who sleeps where, who accompanies, who drives.
5. **How do adults communicate with under-18 athletes outside training?** Individual messages, or a group that includes parents: it is the easiest rule to write and the most often missing.
6. **What happens to a report?** Who receives it, how quickly they respond, who is informed.

None of these measures currently has proof of effectiveness from a controlled trial: the IOC consensus recommends them on the basis of the risk model and the experience of national systems, not of experiments ([Tuakli-Wosornu et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2024-108766)). They should be presented for what they are: reasonable, near-zero-cost practices that make difficult what is currently easy.

## What can a coach do next week?

Four things, all free, all visible to the group:

- **Drop physical exercise from the punishment repertoire.** It is an explicit item in the physical violence questionnaires, and this is not about being strict: it is about normalising the use of athletes' bodies as a disciplinary tool.
- **Stop commenting on bodies**, including positively, including with good intentions. "You've grown", "you're back in shape", "you won't run like that" are sentences athletes remember for years; on the effect of [a coach's words](/en/blog/parole-allenatore-salute-atlete) this blog has a dedicated article.
- **Never be alone with an under-18 athlete** in a closed space — changing room, office, car — when it can be avoided, and when it cannot, tell someone before and after. It protects the coach as much as the athlete.
- **Say out loud, at the start of the season, who to turn to.** 25.3% of those who did not seek help did not know who to ask: it is the easiest share in the whole study to bring to zero.

## Who to turn to

Inside sport: the club's **Responsabile contro abusi, violenze e discriminazioni** and the federation's *safeguarding officer*, who receive reports even when they concern registered members.

Outside sport, in Italy: **114 Emergenza Infanzia** and Telefono Azzurro's **19696** line, both free and staffed around the clock, for minors and for adults who want to report a situation. Where a criminal offence may have occurred, the report goes to the police.

If the athlete is unwell — sleep, mood, anxiety, a sudden refusal to go to training — the first medical contact remains the paediatrician or family doctor, who can refer to a mental health professional. On telling tiredness, [performance anxiety and burnout](/en/blog/ansia-prestazione-burnout-giovani-atlete) apart, this blog has a separate article: none of those signals, on its own, tells you what happened.

## BAB's role

On this subject BAB is not a reporting channel and does not make assessments: pretending otherwise would be dangerous. The channels are the ones above.

What BAB offers is a **private space for the athlete**, where a girl records her own signals over time — load, energy, mood, sleep, pain — and sees the patterns emerge. Having her own record helps here too, for a simple reason: it makes a lasting change visible to her first, and gives words to a feeling that otherwise stays "I don't feel like it". Clubs receive **only aggregated, anonymous data**, never an individual athlete's diary.

The educational half is what this article tries to do: to say that 46.5% of those who did not seek help thought it was **tolerable**, and that this threshold — not the severity of extreme cases — is the thing a club can actually move.

## When to consult a professional

This article is educational and describes population-level data: it cannot establish what happened to an individual athlete, and it is not an assessment tool. If an athlete describes an episode, the first step is not to investigate but to **listen without interrogating**, and to activate the channels above. If signs of distress persist — disturbed sleep, anxiety, withdrawal from relationships, falling school performance, refusing to train — the contact point is the paediatrician or family doctor, who can refer to a mental health professional.
