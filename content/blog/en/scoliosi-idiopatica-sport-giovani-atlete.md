It often happens like this: during a check-up with the paediatrician, or because someone notices one shoulder sitting higher in a swimsuit, a girl of 12 or 13 is told she has **scoliosis**. The question that follows is almost always the same: and what about sport?

The short answer is that, in the great majority of cases, sport should not be taken away, and international guidelines explicitly recommend keeping it — including during brace treatment. The long answer is more interesting, because the relationship between sport and scoliosis is full of inherited beliefs: that gymnastics causes it, that swimming cures it, that a curve makes the back fragile. The data confirm none of the three, at least not in that form.

This article lays out what we know, with the population it was measured in, and what a sports club can do without making diagnoses.

> **In short**
> - **Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis** is a sideways curve of the spine of **at least 10°**, measured with the **Cobb angle**, with no identifiable cause, appearing between **10 and 18** and tending to worsen during growth (USPSTF, 2018).
> - It affects **1-3%** of 10-16-year-olds (Weinstein et al., 2008). In curves **above 40°** the female-to-male ratio rises to **7.2:1** (Konieczny et al., 2013; school-screening data with inconsistent methods).
> - **Rhythmic gymnastics and ballet** are the sports most associated with it: **12% against 1.1%** in 100 rhythmic gymnasts compared with non-sporting peers (Tanchev et al., 2000). But the direction of the association is **uncertain**, and there is a **selection** hypothesis linked to joint laxity (Newman et al., 2023; Meyer et al., 2006).
> - **Swimming is not a treatment**: in 112 competitive swimmers aged 12.5, trunk asymmetries were more likely than in their peers (**OR 1.86**; Zaina et al., 2015).
> - Those who do **more vigorous activity** are less often diagnosed with scoliosis (**OR 0.76**, 16 observational studies; Newman et al., 2023): an association, not a cause.
> - The **2016 SOSORT guidelines** recommend **continuing sport**, including **with a brace**, and **not prescribing it as a treatment** (Negrini et al., 2018).
> - A brace works in proportion to the hours it is worn: **72% against 48%** of curves staying below 50° in the BrAIST trial (Weinstein et al., 2013). Time out of the brace for training is agreed with whoever prescribes it.
> - After **spinal fusion** most patients return to sport **within a year**, usually between **8 and 12 months** (Nassar et al., 2026).

## What is adolescent idiopathic scoliosis, and how is it measured?

**It is a sideways curve of the spine of at least 10°, with no identifiable cause, appearing between the ages of 10 and 18.** «Idiopathic» means exactly that: it is not due to malformed vertebrae or to a neurological or muscular disease, and its origin remains unknown. It is the most common form of scoliosis ([USPSTF, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.19342)).

It is not simply a «crooked» back seen from behind, though. The guidelines describe it as a **three-dimensional** deformity: the spine bends sideways and, at the same time, the vertebrae rotate ([Negrini et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13013-017-0145-8)). It is the rotation that produces the most visible sign: when the girl bends forward, one side of her back sits higher than the other.

Two measurements, with two different jobs:

- the **Cobb angle** is measured on a standing X-ray of the spine, and it is the number that makes the diagnosis: below 10° we speak of asymmetry, not scoliosis;
- the **Adams forward bend test** — bending the trunk forward with straight legs, with a **scoliometer** placed on the back — measures trunk rotation and is a **screening** tool: it tells you whether it is worth looking further, not whether scoliosis is present. In the Norwegian school screening programme the threshold for referral to X-ray was a rib hump **above 7°** ([Adobor et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1186/1748-7161-6-23)).

For coaches, the fact that matters is that the curve **tends to worsen during growth**, before the skeleton has finished maturing ([USPSTF, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.19342)). The years of [peak height velocity](/en/blog/picco-di-crescita-giovani-atlete) are also the years in which a small curve can become one that needs treatment.

## How many girls are affected, and why more than boys?

**Between 10 and 16 it affects 1-3% of boys and girls, but as the curve grows it becomes an almost exclusively female issue.** The 1-3% estimate comes from the review published in *The Lancet* ([Weinstein et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(08)60658-3)); a review of school-screening studies reports a wider range, from **0.47% to 5.2%**, because ages, methods and diagnostic criteria change from one study to the next ([Konieczny et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11832-012-0457-4)).

The sex difference is more stable. Overall the female-to-male ratio ranges from **1.5:1 to 3:1**, but it depends on curve size: it is **1.4:1** in curves of 10-20° and rises to **7.2:1** in curves above 40° ([Konieczny et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11832-012-0457-4)). Put simply: mild curves are spread almost evenly; the curves that end up needing treatment are, in large majority, girls' curves.

One figure from a European population gives the scale. In **4,000 Norwegian twelve-year-olds** screened at school, radiographically confirmed scoliosis — a curve above 10° — affected **0.55%**, and only **0.13%** had a curve above 20° ([Adobor et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1186/1748-7161-6-23)). The same study carries a useful warning: a single check at age 12 **was not enough** to identify the girls who would need a brace, and in one of them the curve progressed to **45° within seven months**, leading to surgery.

## Do gymnastics, ballet and swimming cause scoliosis?

**Rhythmic gymnastics and ballet are associated with more frequent scoliosis; that they cause it has not been shown, and part of the explanation is probably selection.** Swimming, contrary to what is often said, does not cure it.

The most-quoted numbers come from **rhythmic gymnastics**. In a study of **100 girls** who practised it, scoliosis affected **12%**, against **1.1%** of non-sporting peers ([Tanchev et al., 2000](https://doi.org/10.1097/00007632-200006010-00008)). The authors describe three recurring features — **generalised joint laxity, delayed maturation and repeated asymmetric loading** of the growing spine — and propose that together they explain the excess. It is a hypothesis, built on a cross-sectional study of a single group.

In **classical ballet** the signal is similar. In **2,600 Japanese junior-high schoolgirls** referred for a second check after screening, classical ballet was associated with scoliosis (**OR 1.38**; 95% CI 1.09-1.75), and the association rose with the frequency, years and duration of training; no lifestyle factor was associated, while a low body mass index and a mother with scoliosis were ([Watanabe et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.16.00459)). In a small Australian study of **30 ballet dancers aged 9-16**, 30% screened positive against 3% of peers who did not dance ([Longworth et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2014.02.027)) — with a scoliometer, not an X-ray.

The most recent meta-analysis, **16 observational studies and 9,627 participants** (95% female), confirms that ballet and gymnastics are the only sports significantly associated with a scoliosis diagnosis, but concludes that **the direction of the association is uncertain** ([Newman et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2023.01.019)).

Why uncertain? Because there is an alternative explanation, with data behind it. In a study of **201 adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis and 192 peers without**, those with scoliosis did gymnastics more often, but **had chosen it before diagnosis**; and in a subgroup of girls, joint laxity was higher in those with scoliosis, **whether or not they did gymnastics** ([Meyer et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2005.00482.x)). The authors' hypothesis is that a common factor — laxity — makes both scoliosis and the pull towards a sport that rewards flexibility more likely. It is the same selection mechanism seen with [joint hypermobility](/en/blog/ipermobilita-articolare-giovani-atlete) in dance.

And **swimming**? It is often recommended «for the back», sometimes precisely as a treatment for scoliosis. In **112 competitive swimmers aged 12.5** compared with **217 students** of the same age, swimming was associated with a higher risk of **trunk asymmetry** (OR 1.86; 95% CI 1.08-3.20) and **hyperkyphosis** (OR 2.26; 95% CI 1.35-3.77), and in girls with more low back pain ([Zaina et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpeds.2014.09.024)). Again: a cross-sectional study, screening measures rather than X-rays. The point is not that swimming is harmful — it is that it is not a therapy, and someone with scoliosis should not choose it in the belief that it will treat her.

## Does sport increase or reduce the risk of scoliosis?

**In the available data, those who do more vigorous physical activity are less often diagnosed with scoliosis.** In the meta-analysis above, a history of vigorous activity reduces the odds of a new diagnosis by **24%** (**OR 0.76**; 95% CI 0.65-0.89), with the certainty of evidence rated high ([Newman et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2023.01.019)). In the UK ALSPAC cohort, among **4,640 boys and girls** assessed at 15, those who did more accelerometer-measured moderate or vigorous activity at 11 were **30% less likely** to have scoliosis at 15 ([Tobias et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyy268)).

These are observational studies, and the ALSPAC study itself warns against reading them as a protective effect of sport: differences in motor ability were already visible at **18 months** in those who later developed scoliosis. Movement may protect; or those who are predisposed may move a little less from early childhood. What the data make hard to sustain is the idea that sport, in general, is a risk factor.

## Does a girl with scoliosis have to stop playing sport?

**No, in the great majority of cases. The guidelines ask for the opposite: to keep it.** The 2016 guidelines of SOSORT, the international scientific society on conservative scoliosis treatment, devote six of their 68 recommendations to sport ([Negrini et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13013-017-0145-8)). The main ones, with their strength:

- sport **should be practised** for the psychological, neuromotor and general well-being benefits it offers (strength B);
- sport **should not be prescribed as a treatment** for scoliosis (strength C);
- depending on curve severity and progression, **a clinician specialised in conservative treatment may restrict** specific types of sport (strength B).

An earlier systematic review reached the same conclusion: scoliosis **is not a contraindication to most sports**, and those treated with a brace or surgery can take part in physical activity at the same level as those who received no treatment ([Green et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcm.2008.11.001)). These recommendations rest largely on low-quality studies and expert opinion, however: they are a shared direction, not proof.

In real life, those with scoliosis do not move less than others. Among **239 adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis** — untreated, currently braced, previously braced or operated — and **58 peers without it**, self-reported physical activity levels were similar, and neither bracing nor surgery was associated with less activity ([Diarbakerli et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13013-016-0082-y)).

The real risk, then, is not sport. It is **taking it away**: at an age when [girls dropping out of sport](/en/blog/abbandono-puberta) is already a problem, a diagnosis can become the reason to stop without any doctor having asked for it.

## Can you play sport while wearing a brace?

**Yes, and the guidelines recommend continuing, with caution in contact or highly dynamic sports.** The SOSORT recommendations are explicit: sport **should be continued during brace treatment** for its physical and psychological benefits (strength B), while **contact or highly dynamic** sports should be practised **with caution** (strength C) ([Negrini et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13013-017-0145-8)). The systematic review adds that athletes can train with the brace on, but also without it ([Green et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcm.2008.11.001)).

There is a practical constraint every club should know, though: **a brace works in proportion to the hours it is worn.** In the BrAIST trial, **242 adolescents** with curves at risk of progression, prescribed at least **18 hours a day**, **72%** of those braced reached the end of growth without passing **50°** — the treatment-failure threshold, close to the indication for surgery — against **48%** of those only observed, and success rose with hours of wear ([Weinstein et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1307337)).

For moderate curves there are other schedules too. In a Swedish randomised trial of **135 patients aged 9-17** (82% girls) with curves of 25-40°, a **night-time brace** combined with physical activity prevented progression in **76%** of cases against **53%** with physical activity alone, while scoliosis-specific exercises did not do significantly better than physical activity alone ([Charalampidis et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2023.52492)).

The consequence for coaches is concrete: **time out of the brace for training is decided with whoever prescribed it**, not in the gym. One extra session a week can matter little or a lot, depending on the programme.

## Can you return to sport after surgery?

**Yes: most girls and boys who have surgery return to sport within a year.** The most recent systematic review, **11 studies and 722 patients** with a mean age of 15.2, finds that return usually happens **between 8 and 12 months** after posterior spinal fusion; it is delayed by older age, larger curves, longer or lower fusions, and psychological factors such as **fear of injury**, anxiety and deconditioning. Some patients move from contact or competitive sports to non-contact or non-competitive ones ([Nassar et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465251390546)).

Timing depends heavily on what the surgeon says. In a small prospective cohort of **26 athletes** cleared for unrestricted activity after **4-8 weeks**, median time to return was **2.7 months**, and at one year 24 of 26 reported being back at their previous level; the most common barriers were conditioning and flexibility, and contact sports took longer ([Tetreault et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43390-022-00592-8)). An earlier review reported return times of **6 to 18 months**, a switch to lower-impact sports in **28-36.6%** of patients, mostly because of reduced spinal flexibility, and no complications related to returning ([Barile et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s43390-021-00330-6)).

Return also has a part that X-rays do not show: the same [fear of getting hurt again](/en/blog/ritorno-allo-sport-dopo-infortunio) seen after injuries, which belongs to the process and is not a whim.

## Hypermobility, bone and periods: how do they fit in?

**They recur in studies of gymnasts and ballet dancers with scoliosis, but the data are few, small and inconsistent.** They are worth knowing as signals, not as explanations.

In **274 gymnasts aged 10-16** — rhythmic, acrobatic and artistic — **28.8%** screened positive for suspected scoliosis with the Adams forward bend test and a scoliometer; suspected scoliosis was associated with **joint laxity**, **lower bone strength**, **higher training volume** and older age, but not with menarche or pubertal stage ([Dar et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-05895-0)). These are screening results, not X-ray diagnoses.

Studies of **adult** dancers bring back the theme of development and energy. In **75 professional ballet dancers** (mean age 24.3) scoliosis affected **24%**, and **83%** of those with scoliosis had reached menarche at 14 or later, against **54%** of those without ([Warren et al., 1986](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM198605223142104)). In a more recent professional company, scoliosis affected **20.4% of the 49 female dancers** against **6.1% of the 49 male dancers**, and female dancers with scoliosis had **less fat mass** ([Lambert et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.24.00670)). The authors link this profile to [low energy availability](/en/blog/red-s-bassa-disponibilita-energetica); but these are cross-sectional studies in adults, and in the small Australian sample of adolescent dancers scoliosis was associated with **neither** hypermobility nor age at menarche ([Longworth et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2014.02.027)).

The cautious reading: late or absent periods, a very lean body and high loads are already [signals to take seriously for bone](/en/blog/salute-ossea-fratture-da-stress-giovani-atlete). That they matter for the spine too is plausible, not proven.

## How do the people around an athlete notice it?

**By looking at her back when she bends forward, and knowing that the diagnosis is made by an X-ray, not an experienced eye.** The SOSORT guidelines recommend that **paediatricians, GPs and sports physicians** perform the Adams forward bend test with a scoliometer **at every visit between the ages of 8 and 15** (strength B) ([Negrini et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13013-017-0145-8)). Not everyone agrees: in the United States the Preventive Services Task Force concluded that the evidence is insufficient to say whether population screening of adolescents does more good than harm ([USPSTF, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.19342)).

The simplest sign is the one the test makes visible: when the athlete bends forward with straight legs, **one side of her back sits higher than the other**. An asymmetry of the shoulders or hips noticed in a swimsuit is also a reasonable reason to ask the paediatrician for a check, without alarm.

Do not rely on the Italian competitive-sport eligibility exam for this: its protocol is built mainly around [cardiac screening](/en/blog/visita-idoneita-sportiva-giovani-atlete) and does not include a specific spinal measurement.

## What can a sports club do without making diagnoses?

Four things, none of which requires clinical expertise.

**Do not take sport away out of caution.** If an athlete arrives with a diagnosis, the question to ask the family is not «can she still train?», but «are there any instructions about what to avoid?». Restrictions, when needed, are decided by the specialist based on the curve and its progression ([Negrini et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13013-017-0145-8)).

**Organise training around the brace, not against it.** Know how many hours a day it has to be worn, whether the athlete trains with it or without it, where she can change discreetly. It is logistics, but it is the logistics that keeps an athlete in the sport.

**Do not comment on her back in front of the team.** «Stand up straight» or «your shoulder is crooked» do not correct scoliosis, and they stay with a thirteen-year-old. On the weight of [a coach's words](/en/blog/parole-allenatore-salute-atlete) the blog has a dedicated article.

**In rhythmic gymnastics and ballet, look at the whole picture, not just the spine.** Training volume, laxity and bone health recur in studies of gymnasts with scoliosis ([Dar et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-05895-0); [Tanchev et al., 2000](https://doi.org/10.1097/00007632-200006010-00008)), and load in the years of [early specialisation](/en/blog/specializzazione-precoce-giovani-atlete) is already a topic in its own right.

## What BAB does here

BAB does not screen and does not measure the spine: a curve is assessed with a clinical examination and an X-ray, not with an app.

What it offers is a **private space for the athlete** in which a girl records her load, energy, sleep, pain and cycle over time, and sees the patterns emerge. For an athlete with scoliosis this means being able to bring something more precise to a check-up than «my back hurts sometimes»: when, after which session, in which weeks. Clubs only ever receive **aggregated, anonymous data**, never an individual athlete's diary.

The educational half is what this article tries to do: say that a scoliosis diagnosis is, almost always, **not the end of sport**, and that removing it as a precaution is a choice with a cost.

## When should you see a professional?

**Whenever you notice an asymmetry of the back, and before deciding anything about sport.** The first port of call is the paediatrician or GP, who performs the Adams forward bend test and, if needed, arranges an X-ray and a specialist referral.

These should be checked without waiting: an asymmetry that seems to be **increasing over a few months**, especially during the growth spurt; **back pain that persists** or limits activity, which in young athletes also has other causes to consider, covered in the article on [back pain](/en/blog/mal-di-schiena-giovani-atlete); and any doubt about training, competition and bracing, which should be agreed with whoever is managing the treatment.

(narrative review; estimates a prevalence of **1-3%** in the at-risk population aged **10-16**; notes that the effectiveness of bracing and physiotherapy had not, at the time, been rigorously assessed) [doi:10.1016/S0140-6736(08)60658-3](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(08)60658-3)
- Konieczny M.R., Senyurt H., Krauspe R. **Epidemiology of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis.** *Journal of Children's Orthopaedics*, 2013;7(1):3-9. The authors warn that the methods, ages and diagnostic criteria of the studies are **not comparable**) [doi:10.1007/s11832-012-0457-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11832-012-0457-4)
- Adobor R.D., Rimeslatten S., Steen H., Brox J.I. **School screening and point prevalence of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis in 4000 Norwegian children aged 12 years.** *Scoliosis*, 2011;6:23. (school screening with the Adams forward bend test and scoliometer, rib hump threshold **>7°**, radiographic confirmation **>10°**; prevalence **0.55%**, curves above 20° **0.13%**; screening at age 12 alone identified no bracing candidates) [doi:10.1186/1748-7161-6-23](https://doi.org/10.1186/1748-7161-6-23)
- US Preventive Services Task Force (Grossman D.C., Curry S.J., Owens D.K., et al.). **Screening for Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis: US Preventive Services Task Force Recommendation Statement.** *JAMA*, 2018;319(2):165-172. (definition: a curve with Cobb angle **≥10°** between **10 and 18**, usually worsening before skeletal maturity; **insufficient** evidence to assess the benefits and harms of population screening in adolescents; adequate evidence that bracing may slow progression. **US context**) [doi:10.1001/jama.2017.19342](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2017.19342)
- Negrini S., Donzelli S., Aulisa A.G., Czaprowski D., Schreiber S., de Mauroy J.C., et al. **2016 SOSORT guidelines: orthopaedic and rehabilitation treatment of idiopathic scoliosis during growth.** *Scoliosis and Spinal Disorders*, 2018;13:3. (**consensus** guidelines with 68 recommendations, **6 on sport**: practise it for its general benefits, strength B; do not prescribe it as treatment, strength C; restrictions at the specialist's discretion based on severity and progression, strength B; continue it during bracing, strength B; caution with contact or highly dynamic sports during bracing, strength C. Also recommends the Adams test with scoliometer at every visit between 8 and 15, strength B. The sport recommendations rest on **low-level** evidence) [doi:10.1186/s13013-017-0145-8](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13013-017-0145-8)
- Tanchev P.I., Dzherov A.D., Parushev A.D., Dikov D.M., Todorov M.B. **Scoliosis in rhythmic gymnasts.** *Spine*, 2000;25(11):1367-1372. (**cross-sectional** study of **100 girls** practising rhythmic gymnastics compared with non-sporting peers; scoliosis **12% vs 1.1%**; delayed menarche and joint laxity common; hypothesis of a «triad» of laxity, delayed maturation and asymmetric loading, **not shown** to be causal) [doi:10.1097/00007632-200006010-00008](https://doi.org/10.1097/00007632-200006010-00008)
- Watanabe K., Michikawa T., Yonezawa I., Takaso M., Minami S., Soshi S., et al. **Physical Activities and Lifestyle Factors Related to Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis.** *Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery*, 2017;99(4):284-294. (**cross-sectional** study of **2,600 Japanese junior-high schoolgirls** referred for a second check after moiré topography screening; scoliosis defined as a curve ≥15° on X-ray; classical ballet **OR 1.38** (95% CI 1.09-1.75), rising with frequency and years of practice; a mother with scoliosis and low body mass index also associated; **all-female sample, already selected by screening**) [doi:10.2106/JBJS.16.00459](https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.16.00459)
- Newman M., Hannink E., Barker K.L. **Associations Between Physical Activity and Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.** *Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation*, 2023;104(8):1314-1330. (systematic review and meta-analysis of **16 observational studies**, **9,627 participants**, 95% female; history of vigorous activity **OR 0.76** (95% CI 0.65-0.89) for a new diagnosis, high certainty; ballet or gymnastics the only sports associated with diagnosis, **direction of association uncertain**; people with scoliosis do less vigorous activity) [doi:10.1016/j.apmr.2023.01.019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apmr.2023.01.019)
- Meyer C., Cammarata E., Haumont T., Deviterne D., Gauchard G.C., Leheup B., et al. **Why do idiopathic scoliosis patients participate more in gymnastics?** *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports*, 2006;16(4):231-236. (questionnaire of **201 adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis and 192 controls**; gymnastics more common in the scoliosis group and **chosen before diagnosis**; joint laxity measured in **42 girls with scoliosis and 21 controls**, higher in girls with scoliosis regardless of gymnastics. **Selection** hypothesis, observational study) [doi:10.1111/j.1600-0838.2005.00482.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2005.00482.x)
- Zaina F., Donzelli S., Lusini M., Minnella S., Negrini S. **Swimming and Spinal Deformities: A Cross-Sectional Study.** *The Journal of Pediatrics*, 2015;166(1):163-167. (**cross-sectional** convenience sample of **112 competitive swimmers** — 62 female — and **217 students**, age 12.5; screening with scoliometer and plumb-line distances, **not X-rays**; trunk asymmetry **OR 1.86** (95% CI 1.08-3.20), hyperkyphosis OR 2.26, hyperlordosis OR 2.24; low back pain 2.1 times more frequent in female swimmers) [doi:10.1016/j.jpeds.2014.09.024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpeds.2014.09.024)
- Tobias J.H., Fairbank J., Harding I., Taylor H.J., Clark E.M. **Association between physical activity and scoliosis: a prospective cohort study.** *International Journal of Epidemiology*, 2019;48(4):1152-1160. (**prospective** UK ALSPAC cohort; **4,640** participants with DXA at 15, **267 (5.8%)** with scoliosis identified by the DXA method; more accelerometer-measured moderate/vigorous activity at 11 associated with **30% lower** odds; motor ability differences already at 18 months. **Observational study**: association, not cause) [doi:10.1093/ije/dyy268](https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyy268)
- Green B.N., Johnson C., Moreau W. **Is physical activity contraindicated for individuals with scoliosis? A systematic literature review.** *Journal of Chiropractic Medicine*, 2009;8(1):25-37. (systematic review, **11 articles** included, **3 level-3b and 8 level-5 studies**; scoliosis is not a contraindication to most sports, grade D; braced or surgically treated patients participate at the same level as non-surgical patients, grade C; exercise possible with or without the brace, grade D. **Low-quality evidence**) [doi:10.1016/j.jcm.2008.11.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcm.2008.11.001)
- Diarbakerli E., Grauers A., Möller H., Abbott A., Gerdhem P. **Adolescents with and without idiopathic scoliosis have similar self-reported level of physical activity: a cross-sectional study.** *Scoliosis and Spinal Disorders*, 2016;11:17. (**cross-sectional** study; **239 adolescents with idiopathic scoliosis** — 88 untreated, 43 previously braced, 36 currently braced, 72 operated — and **58 controls**; at least moderate activity in 75% vs 85%, not significant; **self-reported** activity via IPAQ questionnaire) [doi:10.1186/s13013-016-0082-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13013-016-0082-y)
- Weinstein S.L., Dolan L.A., Wright J.G., Dobbs M.B. **Effects of Bracing in Adolescents with Idiopathic Scoliosis.** *New England Journal of Medicine*, 2013;369(16):1512-1521. - Charalampidis A., Diarbakerli E., Dufvenberg M., Jalalpour K., Ohlin A., Ahl A.A., et al. **Nighttime Bracing or Exercise in Moderate-Grade Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis: A Randomized Clinical Trial.** *JAMA Network Open*, 2024;7(1):e2352492. *This article is for information and education only and does not constitute medical advice, diagnosis or treatment guidance. It describes data measured in different populations — screened schoolgirls, gymnasts, adolescent and adult ballet dancers, patients under treatment — with the limitations stated in the text. A back asymmetry, persistent back pain or any decision about sport, competition and bracing should be discussed with the paediatrician, GP or the specialist managing the treatment.*
