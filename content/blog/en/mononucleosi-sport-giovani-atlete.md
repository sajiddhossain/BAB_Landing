There is a moment, after mononucleosis, when everyone at the gym asks the same question: **when can she come back?** The fever has gone, the throat no longer hurts, the girl says she feels fine. Starting again seems reasonable.

The trouble is that the answer depends on none of those three things. It depends on an organ that cannot be seen, does not hurt and, usually, sends no signal: **the spleen**. That is why mononucleosis — glandular fever, as it is also known — is, of all the common infections of adolescence, the one that demands most attention from the people who run sport.

And it arrives in exactly the wrong years. Mononucleosis peaks between **15 and 24** (Becker and Smith, 2014): the years of competition, trials and championships that "can't be missed". This article explains what happens in the body, what the documented timelines are, why a single scan is not enough to decide, and what to do about tiredness that sometimes does not go away. With the limit stated up front: **the return is decided by a doctor**, not by an article and not by a coach.

> **In brief**
> - Mononucleosis is most common between **15 and 24**; in the United States about **500 cases per 100,000** people per year (Becker and Smith, 2014).
> - The Epstein-Barr virus is spread through **saliva** and has an incubation period of **30-50 days** (Becker and Smith, 2014).
> - Spleen injuries cluster in the **first 21 days** of illness and are **exceedingly rare after day 28**; rupture occurs in **under 0.5%** of cases (Becker and Smith, 2014).
> - Most recommendations call for **3 weeks** off and then a **gradual** return, but **there are no guidelines based on controlled studies** (Becker and Smith, 2014; Putukian et al., 2008).
> - In **85** published cases of splenic rupture, the presenting symptom was **abdominal pain in 88%**, and only **14%** had any trauma (Bartlett et al., 2016).
> - In **631 healthy collegiate athletes**, more than **7%** had an "enlarged" spleen at baseline: a single scan says little (Hosey et al., 2006).
> - In **301 adolescents** aged 12-18, chronic fatigue after mononucleosis affected **13% at 6 months** and **4% at 24 months**; everyone still affected at 2 years was **female** (Katz et al., 2009).

## What is mononucleosis, and why does it arrive at that age?

Infectious mononucleosis is caused in the vast majority of cases by the **Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)**, a relative of the herpesviruses that, once inside, stays in the body for life in a latent form. It spreads through **saliva** — hence its nickname, the "kissing disease" — and has a long incubation period of **30-50 days** (Becker and Smith, 2014). In practice: by the time symptoms show, it is almost impossible to say where or when the infection was caught.

The virus is extremely common: **90-95%** of adults carry antibodies. What makes the difference is the age at which it is met. In childhood the infection often goes unnoticed; when it arrives for the first time in **adolescence** or young adulthood, it more often causes the full illness. Hence the peak between **15 and 24**, estimated in the United States at about **500 cases per 100,000 people** per year, with **no difference** between males and females (Becker and Smith, 2014). These are US figures from a clinical review: they give an order of magnitude, not a precise estimate for any other country.

No study suggests athletes catch it more often than their peers. The issue for sport is not frequency: it is that athletes **take more knocks**, and with an enlarged spleen knocks matter.

## How do you recognise it in an athlete?

The classic picture is well described (Becker and Smith, 2014):

1. **3-5 days** of malaise, fatigue and loss of appetite;
2. then the **triad**: sore throat, fever, swollen neck glands (especially at the back of the neck);
3. in about **a third** of cases, small red spots on the **palate**, a highly suggestive sign.

Two practical details. The sore throat of mononucleosis is often mistaken for strep throat — and the two infections can also **coexist**, in up to 30% of cases. A skin rash appears in **10-40%** of patients, more often in those who have been given a penicillin for a presumed bacterial infection (Becker and Smith, 2014): one of the reasons the diagnosis matters, and why this is not something to sort out at home with leftover antibiotics.

But in an athlete the presentation can be far more subtle: **nothing more than fatigue, lack of energy or a drop in performance** (Becker and Smith, 2014). That sentence is worth remembering at the gym, where an unexplained dip is easily read as a lack of effort, or as a shortage of [iron](/en/blog/ferro-atlete-adolescenti) or of [energy](/en/blog/red-s-bassa-disponibilita-energetica) — legitimate hypotheses, but not the only ones.

### What about the rapid test?

The rapid test (the so-called *monospot*) looks for antibodies that appear with a delay: about **85-90%** of confirmed cases test positive **by the third week**, but in the **first week** false negatives can reach **25%** (Becker and Smith, 2014). A negative test in the first few days does not close the question: sometimes it needs repeating, or replacing with more specific tests. That is a decision for the doctor.

## Why is the spleen the real problem?

The spleen sits in the upper left of the abdomen, normally protected by the lower ribs. In mononucleosis lymphocytes infiltrate it and **enlarge** it — splenomegaly —: it moves out from under the rib cage and also becomes structurally **more fragile**, to the point that it can rupture **spontaneously** or after minor trauma (Becker and Smith, 2014).

The timelines are the most useful data in this whole article:

| What | When | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peak spleen enlargement | usually **within 2 weeks**, occasionally up to **3.5 weeks** | Becker and Smith, 2014 |
| Return to normal size | in most cases within **4-6 weeks** | Becker and Smith, 2014 |
| Spleen injuries | mostly in the **first 21 days**; **exceedingly rare after day 28** | Becker and Smith, 2014 |
| Frequency of rupture | **under 0.5%** of cases | Becker and Smith, 2014 |
| Mean time to rupture in published cases | **14 days**, with cases up to **8 weeks** | Bartlett et al., 2016 |

Half a per cent sounds small, and it is. But in a review of **85 published cases** of splenic rupture during mononucleosis, **overall mortality was 9%**, and two thirds of patients had their spleen removed (Bartlett et al., 2016). A rare, serious event is exactly the kind of risk for which a cautious rule makes sense even when "it probably won't happen".

The same review holds a second, perhaps more important, finding: only **14%** of cases had any **trauma**. In the great majority the spleen ruptured **without an identifiable blow**, and the presenting symptom was **abdominal pain in 88%** of cases (Bartlett et al., 2016). The sample — case reports, mean age 22, 70% male — cannot be used to estimate risk in an adolescent. But the practical message holds at any age: **tummy pain in a girl with mononucleosis, or in the weeks after it, is an emergency**, even if nobody touched her.

Contact sports are therefore not the only thing that matters. Activities that raise **pressure inside the abdomen** — lifting weights, straining with a held breath — are also listed among those to avoid during the risk phase (Becker and Smith, 2014).

## Why is a single scan not enough?

Because the spleen cannot be felt by hand, and the image has nothing to compare against.

**Physical examination** is unreliable: in one study cited by the reference review it detected enlargement in as few as **17%** of cases (Becker and Smith, 2014). **Ultrasound** is the imaging of choice, but it has a structural limit: nobody knows how big that athlete's spleen was before the illness.

The study that showed this best scanned the spleens of **631 healthy collegiate athletes**. Mean length was **10.65 cm**, but with wide variation; men had larger spleens than women, and more than **7%** of **healthy** athletes already met the conventional criteria for enlargement (Hosey et al., 2006). The authors' conclusion is blunt: **a single ultrasound** to decide whether the spleen is enlarged is of **limited value**.

This is a population of young **adults**, not adolescents, and at that age the spleen is still growing. But the logic holds: **repeated measurements over time** say far more than a single one, and that is how ultrasound is used when the doctor considers it helpful.

## When can she return to training?

The uncomfortable part first: **there are no guidelines based on controlled studies** (Putukian et al., 2008; Becker and Smith, 2014). There is a practical consensus built on the spleen's timeline, and a recommendation to **decide case by case**.

The pathway described by the reference review is this (Becker and Smith, 2014):

1. **First condition, non-negotiable: no symptoms.** No fever, no sore throat, no marked fatigue. Many girls do not feel like moving in the first weeks anyway, which simplifies things.
2. **Around 3 weeks from symptom onset** before starting again: this is the window in which the spleen risk is concentrated. Strict **bed rest** is not needed; avoiding sport is.
3. **A gradual return**: very light activity first, such as **walking**, then light aerobic work, building progressively. There is no evidence that an early return **to light activity** does harm.
4. **Contact, collisions and breath-held efforts last**, with an assessment that considers the type of sport and the point in the season. Faced with ruptures up to 8 weeks after onset, some authors suggest avoiding high-risk sport for **8 weeks** or confirming by ultrasound that the enlargement has resolved (Bartlett et al., 2016).

And one figure that helps manage expectations: the acute phase usually lasts **2-3 weeks**, but **full recovery can take 2-3 months** (Becker and Smith, 2014). Getting back to training and getting back to form are two different milestones, and the second comes later.

Anyone who has read [how return works after a concussion](/en/blog/commozione-cerebrale-giovani-atlete) will recognise the structure: conditions to meet before starting, steps climbed one at a time, and a final "yes" that belongs neither to the athlete nor to the coach. The general principle is the same as for [any return to sport](/en/blog/ritorno-allo-sport-dopo-infortunio): the calendar is not a criterion.

## What if the tiredness does not lift?

For most girls, it does. But not for all of them — and adolescents, girls in particular, are exactly the group in which this has been measured best.

In a prospective study of **301 adolescents aged 12 to 18** with mononucleosis, paediatric criteria for **chronic fatigue syndrome** were met by **13%** at 6 months, **7%** at 12 months and **4%** at 24 months. Most recovered with time; but **all 13 adolescents** still meeting the criteria at 24 months were **female**, and they already reported more severe fatigue at 12 months (Katz et al., 2009).

Two caveats, so this number does not turn into a fear:

- it is **one** cohort from a single US centre, and chronic fatigue syndrome has precise diagnostic criteria: it is not the same as simply "feeling a bit tired";
- the reference review states that **exercise itself does not appear to raise** the risk of chronic fatigue; the warning is rather not to force a full return until the girl genuinely **feels well** (Becker and Smith, 2014).

Tiredness that lasts weeks or months after mononucleosis should therefore go to a doctor, not be handled with more load or a talk about motivation. And [sleep](/en/blog/sonno-atlete-adolescenti), in those weeks, is part of recovery, not an optional extra.

## And the squad? Is it contagious?

The virus spreads through saliva, and after infection it keeps being **shed in saliva**, declining over the **first year** — and it stays in the body for life (Becker and Smith, 2014). Keeping an athlete away from the squad is therefore not what protects her teammates: many will already have met the virus, and anyone who has just had it keeps shedding it for a long time anyway.

The useful rule is a different one, and it applies always, not just with mononucleosis: **water bottles, cups and cutlery are not shared**. The reason to stay off the pitch in the first weeks is **the spleen**, not contagion.

## What a coach can do (and what not)

**You can:**

- **refuse a return "by feel"** in the first 3 weeks, even if the girl says she is fine: the risk window for the spleen cannot be felt;
- **prepare a gradual return**: walking, light aerobic work, then increasing loads, with **contact and breath-held efforts last**, after the doctor's go-ahead;
- **know that abdominal pain is an alarm signal** — even without a knock, even weeks later — and that it means calling emergency services, not waiting for the end of the session;
- **take lasting tiredness seriously** and flag it to the family, without reading it as a lack of commitment;
- **remember that [the sports eligibility exam](/en/blog/visita-idoneita-sportiva-giovani-atlete)** is the natural moment to pick the conversation up again with the sports physician, if the illness was significant.

**You cannot:**

- decide the return based on the fixture list;
- suggest medicines or antibiotics "to speed things up": in mononucleosis there is no role for antibiotics or antivirals, and some common medicines are advised against (Becker and Smith, 2014) — that is for the doctor;
- ask the athlete to "see how it goes" in a match.

In the great majority of girls, mononucleosis is an illness that passes and leaves nothing behind. The job of the adults around her is to make sure it passes **without incident**, respecting a window of a few weeks whose risk the athlete herself cannot feel.
