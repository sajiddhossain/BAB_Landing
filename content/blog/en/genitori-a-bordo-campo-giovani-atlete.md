Nobody teaches a parent how to behave on the sideline. You learn by watching other parents, who learned by watching other parents, and the result is an oral tradition made of unverified good intentions: cheer loudly, offer a few useful instructions, defend your daughter if the referee gets it wrong, and then in the car go over the game while it is still fresh.

Almost every line of that programme, when researchers asked the athletes directly, turned out to be wrong.

Not for moral reasons — none of these behaviours comes from ill will — but for a much simpler one: the person on the pitch and the person in the stands are having two different experiences of the same game, and neither of them knows it. This article gathers what girls said when somebody asked them, and tries to be precise about something that is usually discussed by instinct.

> **In brief**
> - Individual interviews with **36 athletes aged 12-15** (mean 13.5) in team sports: **all 36** ask that parents encourage the **whole team**, focus on **effort** rather than the result, and **control their own emotions**.
> - The rejected behaviours: **do not coach from the sideline** (29 of 36), **do not argue with officials** (31 of 36), **do not draw attention** to yourself or your daughter (28 of 36).
> - **Before** the game two things are wanted: **practical** help (30 of 36) and understanding that many **do not want to talk about the game** before playing it (33 of 36).
> - **After**: feedback that is **positive but honest**, in the sequence good things → things to work on → good things, and **when she decides** (33 of 36).
> - The presence of **both parents** was associated with **higher pre-competitive anxiety** in 341 young athletes — but their **absence** was **not** associated with **lower** anxiety. That is not a reason to stay away.
> - Across 69 youth hockey games the average was **105 spectator comments per game**, mostly positive: the problem is not the average tone, it is the volume.

## What happens when you ask the athletes directly?

Something rare in youth sport research happens: the answers converge.

Three researchers at the University of Alberta individually interviewed **36 athletes aged 12 to 15** (mean age 13.5, standard deviation 1.0), all competing in team sports at least once a month: soccer (12), volleyball (10), basketball (9), ringette (2), softball (2) and ice hockey (1). Each interview lasted 35 minutes on average and asked one thing: what do you want your parents to do — before, during and after competition ([Knight, Neely and Holt, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2010.525589))?

The methodological feature that makes this study useful is the **count**. For every theme, the authors report how many athletes raised it spontaneously. And three themes were raised by **36 athletes out of 36**.

Before reading which, it is worth fixing the limits of the evidence, because they matter as much as the finding. This is a **qualitative** study: it describes what the girls prefer, it does not measure the effect of parental behaviour on performance, anxiety or drop-out. There were 36 participants, recruited at summer camps run by a university in Western Canada, so girls already inside a structured sporting pathway. None of the sentences below is a law. They are requests expressed with surprising agreement by a small, well-defined group of athletes the same age as the girls BAB works with.

## What do all of them ask for, without exception?

The three unanimous requests are these.

**Cheer for the whole team, not only for your daughter** (36 of 36). The reasons given are of two kinds. The first is about climate: when parents know and encourage everyone, the team perceives collective support, and a girl whose family could not make it that week still has somebody cheering for her. The second is subtler and concerns the daughter herself: cheering aimed at one person exposes her, and exposure is precisely what girls this age work to avoid.

**Focus on effort, not on the result** (36 of 36). The athletes describe the consequence concretely: when parents show they care about effort more than the scoreline, perceived pressure drops and the game goes back to being a game. When attention sits on the outcome, the experience changes sign — the study includes an account from a girl who links her father's criticism at the final whistle to no longer wanting to go to her sport.

**Keep your own emotions under control** (36 of 36). This is not «do not feel anything»: the athletes want their parents to come, to watch, to clap and to show interest. What they ask is that extreme emotions — positive as much as negative — stay in check. The line they draw is between a parent who is present and a parent who becomes an event in their own right.

A fourth request is almost as widespread: **interact positively throughout the game** (34 of 36), with a detail worth reporting — the moment athletes say they most need to hear their parents is not when they are winning, it is when they are losing.

## Why is «giving instructions from the sideline» the advice that works least?

Because it puts your daughter in a conflict she cannot resolve while playing.

**29 of 36 athletes** asked parents not to coach during games ([Knight, Neely and Holt, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2010.525589)). The explanations gathered are not about wounded pride, they are about information traffic: sideline instructions contradict the coach's, and the athlete ends up choosing whom to obey in the middle of a passage of play. The cost is not theoretical — it is reaction time spent deciding instead of playing.

The same result appears in a different, younger sample. In an American study, **57 children aged 7-14** (28 boys and 29 girls, mean age 9.7) described how parents behave at competitions and how they would like them to behave. The analysis produced three «roles»: the **supportive parent**, the **demanding coach** and the **crazed fan**. Giving instructions during play falls into the second role, tolerated only in limited circumstances ([Omli and Wiese-Bjornstal, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/02701367.2011.10599807)). That sample is younger and mixed-sex: it shows the phenomenon is not unique to adolescent girls, it does not quantify it in them.

There is, however, an exception, described precisely. If the athlete perceives genuine expertise in the parent, or a good grasp of the team's goals, a technical instruction can help — on four conditions: that it is aimed **only at their own daughter**, that it comes **before or after** the game rather than during, that it **reinforces** rather than contradicts the coach, and that it is offered as a **suggestion**, not as an expectation to be met.

It is worth noting that the same preference had already emerged among **42 tennis players aged 12-15**: no technical or tactical advice, yes to comments on effort and attitude and to practical advice ([Knight, Boden and Holt, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2010.495324)).

## Does arguing with the referee actually protect a girl?

No: according to the players, it exposes her.

**31 of 36 athletes** named parents arguing with officials as a distraction and a source of embarrassment ([Knight, Neely and Holt, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2010.525589)). The least intuitive part is **towards whom** that embarrassment is felt: not only towards the other spectators, but towards the team. The athletes describe having to answer for their parent's behaviour in front of their team-mates — a responsibility nobody asked them to take on, and one that continues in the changing room afterwards.

In the younger American sample, arguing with officials, blaming officials for the result and yelling all fall within the «crazed fan» role, the one children reject most consistently ([Omli and Wiese-Bjornstal, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/02701367.2011.10599807)). That study identifies a single form of raised voice the children considered justified: the **protective** one, when play turns dangerous and somebody is at real risk of getting hurt.

## How much noise is there really on the sideline?

More than it seems to the people making it — and this is the part of the picture that explains why the problem exists even when nobody misbehaves.

In a naturalistic observation in a large Canadian city, five observers attended **69 youth hockey games** and coded spectator comments: an average of **105 comments per game**, mostly positive in tone and directed at players ([Bowker et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1123/tsp.23.3.301)). Another group observed **120 hours** of youth soccer with players aged 10-14 ([Holt et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2007.08.001)); in the summary Knight and colleagues give of that work, roughly a third of comments were praise or encouragement and another third were instructions, while negative and derogatory comments were a smaller share.

The correct reading of these numbers is not «parents are a problem». It is that the **volume** is high, that the share of technical instruction is far from marginal, and that across hundreds of sentences it only takes a few badly calibrated ones for a girl to carry them home. The average tone is benign; the individual experience depends on the exceptions.

## Does attending games increase anxiety?

Here the data are more awkward than the usual summary suggests, and it is worth reporting them in full.

In a French study of **341 young athletes** — 201 in basketball and 140 in tennis — surveyed **before an official competition**, the presence of **both** parents was associated with higher pre-competitive anxiety in every subgroup except male tennis players. But in the same study the **absence** of both parents was **not** associated with lower anxiety ([Bois, Lalanne and Delforge, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640410903062001)).

The two sentences have to be read together, because taken separately each licenses an opposite and equally wrong conclusion. The first alone would suggest staying away; the second shows that removing the presence does not remove the anxiety. What does vary, within the same study, is **how** you are present: directive behaviours and pressure were associated with more pre-competitive anxiety, while praise and understanding were associated with **less** — and that last result, among tennis players, held for the girls only.

Two necessary caveats. This is a **cross-sectional** study: it photographs associations at a single moment and does not show that one behaviour causes the other. And the sample covers boys and girls in two sports, with results that differ by sport and by sex — which is itself the useful information: there is no single «parent in the stands» effect.

## After the game: when is the right moment to talk?

The one she chooses, not the one the engine starts at.

**33 of 36 athletes** described their preferences on post-game feedback, and the picture is more demanding than you might expect ([Knight, Neely and Holt, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/10413200.2010.525589)). They want a **positive** response, yes, but explicitly an **honest** one: they valued parents not saying it had gone well when it had gone badly — being supportive «but not to the extent of lying to you», in one interviewee's words. The structure they describe is a sequence: what went well, then a couple of things to work on, then something positive again.

On **when**, two practical findings. First: immediately after the game, before the team disperses, positive comments are welcome — including from team-mates' parents, which the athletes say carries particular weight. Any constructive criticism, by contrast, should be delivered away from others. Second: athletes ask parents to **read** them and to accept a postponement. The study records the explicit request to be able to say «can we talk about it later?» and be heard. Many also ask for time **with the team** first: the huddle after the game is part of the game.

And this is exactly where the most relevant and least known piece of research sits. An Australian qualitative study of **86 parents and children** in junior Australian football, based on focus groups and individual interviews, looked specifically at the **post-game debrief** — what happens as you leave the ground and on the way home — noting that it is a moment largely ignored by research and central to the experience of the people playing ([Elliott and Drummond, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/13573322.2015.1036233)). It is a study of junior Australian football, so a predominantly male sample in a specific sporting context: it describes a mechanism, not a measure that transfers to girls elsewhere.

The mechanism, though, is recognisable everywhere. The car ride is the first moment parent and daughter are alone, the game is still on both of them, and one of the two has just spent an hour running. It is not a good moment for technical analysis. It is an excellent moment for a single question — «how was it for you?» — and for a willingness to accept silence as an answer.

## Why «having fun» is not a vague word

Because it has been broken down and measured.

One study asked **142 soccer players, 37 coaches and 57 parents** to list everything that makes playing sport fun, then to sort and rate the ideas collected. The result is a map of **11 dimensions of fun** made up of **81 distinct determinants** ([Visek et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1123/jpah.2013-0180)). Fun, in other words, is not the opposite of hard work, nor a concession to younger children: it is an articulated construct that includes learning, improving, belonging to a group and being treated well.

This matters to a parent for a precise reason. The very things athletes ask for on the sideline — cheering for the whole team, attention to effort, no pressure on the result — are not requests to lower the bar. They are the conditions under which the bar can be raised without somebody quitting. And quitting, in this age band, is not a theoretical risk: among girls registered with a club at 10-14, the share who leave sport without ever returning is [71%](/en/blog/abbandono-puberta).

## What a parent can do from next Sunday

Six behaviours, all taken directly from what the athletes asked for.

- **Before:** handle the logistics (30 of 36 ask for it) and say *«do you want to talk about it, or would you rather think about something else?»* instead of assuming that talking helps (33 of 36 raised mental preparation).
- **During — cheer for everyone.** Team-mates' names, not only your daughter's. It is the study's most unanimous request.
- **During — comment on effort, not on the scoreline.** «Great pressure on that ball» works; «you should have won that» shifts the weight onto an outcome she does not control alone.
- **During — no technical instructions.** If there is something useful to say, there is a moment for it: before or after, to her alone, in line with the coach, as a proposal.
- **During — the referee is not a conversational partner.** Thirty-one of 36 athletes said they are the ones who pay for that argument, in embarrassment, in front of the team.
- **After — let her decide when.** The team first, the game second. And if the answer to «how did it go?» is «I do not want to talk about it», that is a complete answer.

None of these points requires sporting expertise. All of them require giving up something that felt useful — and that, not ill will, is the hard part.

## BAB's role

On this subject BAB does one thing, and it is worth stating plainly: it does **not** assess parents, does not collect judgements about their behaviour and does not produce reports on any family.

What it offers is a **private space for the athlete**, where a girl records her own signals over time — load, energy, mood, pain — and sees patterns emerge. If her worst weeks consistently coincide with away games, with certain kinds of match or with periods of particular pressure, that pattern becomes visible to her first. Clubs receive **aggregated, anonymous data only**, never an individual girl's diary.

The educational half is the other contribution: giving a family precise words for something rarely discussed, so that «my dad argues with the referee» can become a conversation between them instead of a silent embarrassment nobody names. It is the same principle that applies to [the words of the person coaching](/en/blog/parole-allenatore-salute-atlete): the frame around a game is not neutral, and it can be changed without asking anybody to love their daughter any less.

## When to consult a professional

The guidance in this article is about behaviour, not mental health. If a girl shows distress that lasts for weeks, if she has stopped enjoying something she used to love, if pre-match anxiety also affects her sleep, her schoolwork and her relationships, or if she talks about quitting in a way that does not sound like a change of interests, that is not something to fix with better cheering: it is the moment to speak to a paediatrician or family doctor, who can point to the right pathway. The blog has a dedicated article on [performance anxiety and burnout](/en/blog/ansia-prestazione-burnout-giovani-atlete).
