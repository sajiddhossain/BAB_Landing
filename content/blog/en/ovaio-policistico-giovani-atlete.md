There is a sentence that works like a lid, in the gym and on the sideline: **"it's just the training"**. A sixteen-year-old's cycle skips a month, then two, then nobody is counting any more — and the explanation arrives before the question does. She trains hard, it's normal.

Sometimes that is exactly right, and it is a serious problem with a name and an article of its own: [low energy availability](/en/blog/red-s-bassa-disponibilita-energetica). But there is a second scenario, told far less often, in which the missing cycle has nothing to do with how much energy is coming in. It has to do with a hormonal axis working **too hard**, not too little. It is **polycystic ovary syndrome** — and in athletes it has been found, in at least one important study, more often than the standard narrative would suggest.

This article lines up three things: how PCOS is recognised in an adolescent (the rules are different from the adult ones, and stricter), why at that age **ultrasound must not be used** for diagnosis, and how the condition is told apart from an athlete's functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea. With one limit stated up front: **none of this is decided by reading an article.** It is decided with a paediatrician, an endocrinologist or a gynaecologist, together with the family.

> **In short**
> - PCOS affects **10-13%** of women of reproductive age, varying with the criteria used (Teede et al., 2023).
> - In **adolescents** the diagnosis requires irregular cycles **and** hyperandrogenism **together**, after excluding other causes (Peña et al., 2020; Teede et al., 2023).
> - **Pelvic ultrasound must not be used** for diagnosis until **8 years post-menarche**, and neither should **AMH**: both overlap with normal pubertal physiology (Teede et al., 2023).
> - "Polycystic" ovarian morphology is found in **up to 40%** of adolescents **2 years** post-menarche, with little long-term clinical relevance (Peña et al., 2025).
> - In **90 Swedish Olympic athletes** (ADULTS), **27%** of non-users of hormonal contraception had menstrual dysfunction, and the most common diagnosis was **PCOS**, not energy deficiency (Hagmar et al., 2009).
> - PCOS and **functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea** are told apart with blood: in the latter, LH, androgens and estradiol are **low** and the LH/FSH ratio is < 1 in about **82%** of cases (Ott et al., 2025).
> - Polycystic ovarian morphology appears in **41.9-46.7%** of women with functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea: that is why ultrasound confuses the picture (Ott et al., 2025).
> - **33.6%** of women with PCOS waited **more than 2 years** and **47.1%** saw **3 or more** professionals before diagnosis (Gibson-Helm et al., 2017).

## What exactly is polycystic ovary syndrome?

It is an **endocrine and metabolic** condition, not a disease of the ovary in any narrow sense. Two elements define it: an **excess of androgens** — hormones present in smaller but not negligible amounts in the female body — and **irregular or absent ovulation**, which shows up as a long, sporadic or vanished cycle.

The name is the first obstacle to understanding it, and the guideline admits as much. What appears on ultrasound are **not cysts**: they are follicles that have stalled at an intermediate stage of development without reaching ovulation. Calling them cysts frightens people and suggests the wrong idea of what is happening.

The prevalence given by the 2023 guideline is **between 10% and 13%** of women of reproductive age (Teede et al., 2023). The range is wide because it depends on which diagnostic criteria are applied: this is a **common** condition, not a rare one, and that alone is an argument for knowing it exists.

## Why are the rules different in an adolescent?

Because at fifteen, many of the signs of PCOS **coincide with normal puberty**. Irregular cycles, some acne, ovaries with many follicles: a healthy girl can have all of these in the first years after menarche, [the same years in which the body changes fastest](/en/blog/picco-di-crescita-giovani-atlete). Applying the adult criteria would mean diagnosing a chronic condition in an enormous number of girls who are simply growing up.

This is why the guideline built criteria that are **stricter**, not looser. In adolescents, **two elements are required together**:

1. **irregular cycles or ovulatory dysfunction**, defined by thresholds that depend on the years elapsed since menarche;
2. **hyperandrogenism**, clinical (moderate-to-severe acne, hirsutism) or biochemical (elevated androgens on good-quality assays).

And only after **excluding** the conditions that mimic PCOS: pregnancy, thyroid dysfunction, hyperprolactinaemia, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing's syndrome, androgen-secreting tumours (Peña et al., 2020; Teede et al., 2023).

What does **not** count towards the criteria in adolescence is as important as what does.

### When is a cycle genuinely irregular?

The thresholds are numerical and worth keeping in mind, because they dismantle both the excessive alarm and the automatic reassurance (Peña et al., 2020; Teede et al., 2023):

| Time since menarche | When the cycle counts as irregular |
| --- | --- |
| Less than 1 year | Irregularity is part of the **normal pubertal transition** |
| 1 to under 3 years | Cycles **< 21 days** or **> 45 days** |
| 3 years onwards | Cycles **< 21 days** or **> 35 days**, or **< 8 cycles** a year |
| Any point after the first year | A **single cycle > 90 days** |

To these is added **primary amenorrhoea**: periods that have never arrived by age **15**, or more than **3 years** after breast development began.

The line that matters most in the daily life of anyone close to an athlete is the last one in the table: **a single cycle beyond 90 days is already abnormal**, regardless of everything else. There is no need to wait for the full picture before asking for an assessment.

### Why ultrasound is not used at that age

This is the counter-intuitive part, and the most useful to know.

The adolescent-specific international recommendations published in 2025 explain the mechanism: what is seen on ultrasound are **arrested follicles**, a normal feature of the maturing ovarian axis, found in **up to 40% of adolescents 2 years post-menarche**, with little clinical relevance in the long term (Peña et al., 2025). A **small** longitudinal analysis of healthy adolescents 2 to 4 years post-menarche had already shown that polycystic ovarian morphology is common and not associated with reproductive dysfunction (Peña et al., 2020).

The operational consequence is unambiguous: **pelvic ultrasound must not be used to diagnose PCOS until 8 years post-menarche**, and the same applies to measuring **anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH)** — both overlap with normal pubertal physiology and would produce over-diagnosis (Teede et al., 2023).

It is worth spelling out what "over-diagnosis" means here: **the label of a chronic condition attached to a healthy girl**, with everything that carries in terms of anxiety, attention to her own body and expectations of herself. In an adolescent, the wrong label is not a neutral error.

### And if not all the criteria are met?

There is a third route, designed for exactly this: the **"at risk" of PCOS** category. It applies to girls who have some features but not all, and it comes with a **planned reassessment** at full reproductive maturity, that is **at or before 8 years post-menarche** (Teede et al., 2023).

It is an elegant solution to a real problem: it allows a girl **not to be lost track of** without having to give her a label today that might not hold tomorrow.

## What has any of this to do with an athlete?

It matters because in athletes an irregular cycle already has a ready explanation, and a ready explanation closes down the search.

The study that challenged that automatism examined **90 Swedish Olympic athletes** across different disciplines. **47%** used hormonal contraception; among the rest, **27%** had menstrual dysfunction, particularly in endurance disciplines. But when the authors looked at body composition and markers of energy availability, they found them **within normal ranges**; there was no osteopenia, and bone mineral density was in fact **elevated**, especially in power athletes. The conclusion was that most of the menstrual disturbances came from **polycystic ovary syndrome**, not from energy-deficiency hypothalamic inhibition (Hagmar et al., 2009).

Two caveats that are part of the finding, not a footnote:

- the sample is of **adult**, **elite** athletes: nobody has shown that the same proportion holds for thirteen- or sixteen-year-olds at a local club;
- the study does **not** say that low energy availability does not exist or does not matter. It says it is not the only explanation, and that the two can be distinguished.

There is also an uncomfortable reading worth naming. Hyperandrogenism can come with a more anabolic body composition, and it is possible that some athletes reach the top partly because of it. Translated: in a share of cases, **the condition goes unseen because the athlete is performing well**. Good performance is not proof that the hormonal axis is healthy.

## How is it told apart from low energy availability?

This is the central clinical question, and the one where ultrasound does the most damage.

The two conditions produce the same visible symptom — a cycle that does not come — starting from **opposite** mechanisms. In **functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea**, the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis is **switched off** by stress, load and [energy deficit](/en/blog/red-s-bassa-disponibilita-energetica). In **PCOS**, the same axis is **overactive**, with increased pulsatile GnRH secretion driving LH and androgen production upwards.

The narrative review in *Human Reproduction Update* devoted to precisely this overlap summarises the markers (Ott et al., 2025):

| | Functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea | PCOS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **LH** | low | elevated |
| **Testosterone and androgens** | low | elevated |
| **Estradiol** | low | not low |
| **AMH** | low | tends to be elevated |
| **SHBG** | tends to be higher | reduced |
| **LH/FSH ratio** | **< 1 in about 82%** of cases | tends to exceed 2 |

And then the figure that on its own explains why ultrasound confuses things: **polycystic ovarian morphology** appears in **41.9-46.7%** of women with functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea — against the 14-33% observed in the general population (Ott et al., 2025). In other words: nearly **one woman in two** whose hormonal axis has been switched off by energy deficit has ovaries that "look" polycystic on a scan. If the reasoning starts from the image, the diagnosis goes the wrong way in one case out of two.

The population in these studies is **adult women**, and the review is narrative, not systematic: it is an orienting picture, not an automatic decision rule.

Why going the wrong way genuinely matters: if a girl with functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea is treated as though she had PCOS, she remains exposed to the consequences of **oestrogen deficiency** — first of all [on bone](/en/blog/salute-ossea-fratture-da-stress-giovani-atlete), in the very years when bone mass is being laid down — and she does not receive the one intervention that would help, which is correcting the energy balance.

## What happens after a diagnosis?

Here the message is less dramatic than the name of the syndrome suggests, and partly good news for anyone who plays sport.

The 2023 guideline recommends **lifestyle interventions** — physical activity alone, or combined with diet and behavioural support — for **everyone** with PCOS, as a first-line strategy. For **adolescents** it specifies at least **60 minutes a day** of moderate- to vigorous-intensity activity, with **muscle- and bone-strengthening activities 3 times a week** (Teede et al., 2023). The *Exercise and Sports Science Australia* position statement reaches the same conclusion and argues it on cardiometabolic, reproductive, psychological and quality-of-life outcomes (Sabag et al., 2024).

For an adolescent athlete already training several times a week, this is usually amply covered. So the message is **not** "train more": it is that a PCOS diagnosis is **not a reason to stop**, and that the sport she is already doing is part of the management, not an obstacle to it.

On [what the cycle actually changes when you train](/en/blog/ciclo-e-performance) there is a dedicated article. On everything else — whether and which medications, how to manage acne or hirsutism, which metabolic checks over time — the answer is an individual clinical pathway, which is not written in an article and not improvised in a changing room. It is worth recalling one point [covered at length elsewhere](/en/blog/contraccezione-ormonale-giovani-atlete), though: the **pill** prescribed "to regulate the cycle" produces a withdrawal bleed that does **not** demonstrate that the hormonal axis is working, and in an athlete it can mask for years whatever lies underneath.

## Why getting there early changes something

Because today, on average, people do not get there early.

In the most cited international survey on the subject, of **1,385 women** with a PCOS diagnosis (53% in North America, 42% in Europe), **33.6%** had waited **more than 2 years** before diagnosis and **47.1%** had consulted **3 or more health professionals**. Only **35.2%** were satisfied with their diagnostic experience, and fewer still with the information they received (Gibson-Helm et al., 2017). It is a sample of **adult** women reconstructing their own path in hindsight: retrospective recall is a declared limitation of the design.

But the point stands, and it is concrete. A diagnosis that arrives late means years in which treatable symptoms go untreated, in which a girl convinces herself that her body works badly without knowing why, and in which metabolic risk factors are not even looked for.

For an athlete there is an extra cost: if an absent cycle is put down to training and left there, **the other diagnosis is missed too** — the energy one — in the cases where that is genuinely what it is. In both directions, "it's just the training" does the same damage: it closes the question before anyone asks it.

## What a coach can do (and cannot)

The boundary matters particularly here, because the clinical signs of PCOS concern **the visible body** of a minor: hair, skin, weight. Those are exactly the things a coach must not comment on.

**You can:**

- **not normalise an absent cycle**, not even when the athlete is performing well — in the Swedish study, elevated bone density and good performance coexisted with menstrual dysfunction (Hagmar et al., 2009);
- **know the thresholds** and use them as thresholds, not as a diagnosis: a cycle beyond 90 days, or never arrived by age 15, deserves a consultation;
- **tell the family** that there is an assessment to be had, and stop there — [how the cycle gets raised with a coach](/en/blog/parlare-di-ciclo-con-allenatore) has rules of its own;
- **know that two opposite routes exist** — a switched-off axis and an overactive one — so as not to repeat to the athlete an explanation nobody has verified.

**You cannot:**

- comment on an athlete's acne, body hair, weight or body shape, in any form and for no "technical" reason whatsoever;
- suggest tests, medications or contraceptives;
- pass on to the squad, to other parents or to anyone not directly involved what an athlete says about her own cycle.

The difference between polycystic ovary syndrome and functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea is established with a blood draw and a clinical examination. All the value a coach can add sits **before** that blood draw: in making sure it happens.
