There is a sentence that circulates in changing rooms, in parents' group chats and sometimes in clinics: **"if her cycle is irregular or missing, the pill will sort it out."** It is nearly always said in good faith. And it is the point at which an adolescent's health problem risks becoming invisible for years.

Because hormonal contraception does many things, and some of them well. But one thing it does not do: **it does not restart the hormonal axis of an athlete who has lost her period, and it does not rebuild the bone she has lost.** On this, the most recent guidelines use no hedging: they recommend **not** using the combined pill to bring menses back or to improve bone density, at the **highest grade of evidence** (Williams et al., 2025).

This article sets out what hormonal contraception does and does not do in sport: for performance, for bones that are still growing, for period pain, for the race calendar. With the population attached to every number — because on this topic the distance between "adult athletes" and "a sixteen-year-old" is the whole difference. And with one limit stated up front: **which method to use, and whether to use one, is not decided by reading an article.** It is decided with a gynaecologist or a paediatrician, with the family.

> **Key points**
> - For **performance** the average effect of the pill is **trivial**: 42 studies and 590 participants, **adult** women; 83% of studies of moderate, low or very low quality; no analysis by age group (Elliott-Sale et al., 2020).
> - The bleed during the pill-free days is a **withdrawal bleed**, not proof that a spontaneous cycle is working. It can **mask** the return of menses (Level B; Williams et al., 2025).
> - For the **bones of an athlete without a period**: in a randomised trial of **121 athletes aged 14-25**, the 17β-estradiol **patch** improved spine and femoral neck Z-scores compared with the **pill** (p=0.011 and p=0.021); the pill was indistinguishable from no treatment (Ackerman et al., 2019).
> - The **2025** guidelines recommend **against** using the combined pill to restore menses or improve bone density: **Level A evidence** (Williams et al., 2025).
> - Adolescence lays down **nearly half** of lifetime bone mass; for those who choose the pill, **30 µg** ethinyl estradiol preparations are preferable to 20 µg ones (Bachrach, 2020).
> - For **period pain** the evidence is "limited" (Peto OR **2.01**; 95% CI 1.32-3.08; I²=64%) and NSAIDs remain the first step in adolescents (Wong et al., 2009; ACOG, 2018a).
> - **60%** of Danish elite athletes on the pill have manipulated their cycle around competitions — but they are **adults** (Oxfeldt et al., 2020).

## What is hormonal contraception, concretely?

**Not one drug, but a family of methods that work in different ways — and that behave differently on bone.** Keeping them apart is the precondition for not confusing the data.

- **Combined pill**: a synthetic oestrogen (usually **ethinyl estradiol**, typically 20 or 30 µg) plus a progestin. During the pill-free or placebo days a **withdrawal bleed** arrives.
- **Progestin-only methods**: the progestin-only pill, the subdermal **implant**, the hormonal **coil** (levonorgestrel intrauterine device), the three-monthly **injection** of depot medroxyprogesterone acetate.
- **Non-oral routes for oestrogen**: patch and ring. Here the distinction matters: oestrogen absorbed through the skin **does not pass through the liver** first, and that changes its effects on the growth factors that matter for bone (Bachrach, 2020).
- **Transdermal estradiol with a cyclic progestin**: this is a **replacement therapy**, not a contraceptive. It looks like a bureaucratic detail and is in fact where most confusion happens — the 2025 guidelines spell it out: it has no contraceptive efficacy (Williams et al., 2025).

A note on long-acting methods: ACOG recommends that implants and coils be **offered to adolescents alongside all other reversible methods**, because in this age group they have higher efficacy, continuation and satisfaction, and it names **the person's own choice** as the principal factor (ACOG, 2018b). That is not a sporting recommendation: it is contraception, and should be read as such.

## Does the pill make performance worse?

**On average no, and the honest answer is: far less than feared, with data that do not cover adolescents.**

The largest systematic review with Bayesian meta-analysis on the topic includes **42 studies and 590 participants** and compares pill users with naturally menstruating women, as well as comparing pill-taking days with withdrawal days. The result: the probability of at least a small effect (d ≥ 0.2) favouring users was **effectively zero**, and the authors conclude that pill use "might result in slightly inferior exercise performance on average", but that any group-level effect is **"most likely to be trivial"** (Elliott-Sale et al., 2020).

Three qualifications that matter more than the headline:

1. **The quality of the evidence is modest.** 83% of included studies were judged of moderate, low or very low quality; only 17% high.
2. **Performance is consistent across the pill cycle.** No difference between taking days and withdrawal days: there is no "worst day" to fear in the blister.
3. **The population is adult women** and the review reports no analysis by age group. There is no equivalent data on girls aged 14-17, and this article does not invent one.

The authors themselves conclude that the evidence **does not warrant a general recommendation** for or against pill use when performance is a priority, and suggest an individualised approach. That is the opposite of what is sometimes said in the gym — "the pill will cost you strength" — and also the opposite of its opposite.

## Is the bleed on the pill a real period?

**No. It is a withdrawal bleed, and this is the single most important distinction in the article.**

On the combined pill, the hormones produced by the ovary are suppressed and replaced by hormones taken from outside. The blood that arrives during the pill-free days is the endometrium's response to the **drop** in those hormones: it is a pharmacological event, not evidence that the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis is working.

The practical problem is that this bleed **looks like** a period, and so it reassures everyone. The 2025 Female Athlete Triad Coalition guidelines make it a recommendation: athletes using the combined pill for contraception should be **cautioned** that it "may mask spontaneous menstrual resumption" and is **not effective in improving bone mineral density**; indeed, "bone loss may continue if the energy deficit state persists" (Level B evidence; Williams et al., 2025).

From which follows a consequence worth writing plainly, because it is counter-intuitive: **amenorrhoea that appears after stopping the pill is not an effect of stopping.** It is what the pill was covering. If a girl stops and her period does not return, the starting point is a clinical assessment — not going back on the pill to "regularise" things.

Anyone who wants to understand what sits underneath a disappearing cycle will find the full picture in the article on [low energy availability (RED-S)](/en/blog/red-s-bassa-disponibilita-energetica): amenorrhoea in an athlete is, in the great majority of cases, a signal of insufficient energy, not a nuisance to switch off.

## Does the pill protect the bones of an athlete who has lost her period?

**No, and here the recent literature is sharper than on almost any other topic on this site.**

The study that weighs most is randomised and includes adolescents. **121 athletes with absent or irregular cycles, aged 14-25**, of normal weight and engaged in weight-bearing activity, were assigned to three arms: a **patch** of 17β-estradiol continuously with cyclic micronised progesterone; a **pill** containing ethinyl estradiol and desogestrel; **no** oestrogen or progestogen. All received calcium and vitamin D.

At 12 months, **bone density Z-scores** at the lumbar spine and femoral neck had increased significantly in the patch group compared with the pill group (p=0.011 and p=0.021) and compared with no treatment (p=0.021 and p=0.033); at the hip, the patch beat the pill (p=0.018). The authors' conclusion: transdermal estradiol improves bone density in young athletes with absent or irregular cycles, **"particularly compared with an ethinyl estradiol-containing contraceptive pill"** (Ackerman et al., 2019).

Read the other way round: **in the pill arm, bone did no better than in the arm with no therapy at all.**

The 2025 guidelines translate this — and other randomised trials — into three recommendations worth reading as written:

- **do not** use the combined pill to re-establish cyclic menses or improve bone density — **Level A evidence**;
- **caution** those using it for contraception that it may mask the return of menses and does not improve bone density — Level B;
- use, as replacement therapy, **transdermal 17β-estradiol at physiologic doses** with a progestin — **Level A evidence** (Williams et al., 2025).

It also confirms a direction already present in the 2014 consensus, which asked that oral contraceptives not be treated as a therapy for an athlete's low bone density (De Souza et al., 2014). Ten years on, with more randomised trials, the grade of evidence has gone up.

One last thing, the most important: **no hormone substitutes for food.** The review on adolescent bone says it without hedging — replacing sex steroids "is ineffective without addressing the energy deficit" and the low IGF-1 that follows from it (Bachrach, 2020). The guidelines accordingly put the nutritional intervention **first**: at least 6-12 months of non-pharmacological treatment, unless bone health deteriorates or new fractures occur (Level C), with a multidisciplinary approach including at least a physician and a dietitian (Level A) (Williams et al., 2025). On why stress fractures are the final bill of this chain, there is [a dedicated article on bone health](/en/blog/salute-ossea-fratture-da-stress-giovani-atlete).

## If her cycle is regular, does the pill change a sixteen-year-old's bones?

**Possibly yes, and that is why the choice belongs with a doctor who knows this girl trains.** This is no longer about amenorrhoea: this is a fifteen- or sixteen-year-old with a normal cycle starting the pill for any reason — contraception, acne, period pain.

The context: adolescence lays down **nearly half of lifetime bone mass** (Bachrach, 2020). It is the window in which even a small effect on bone accrual can matter for a long time.

The most cited data come from a study of **67 adolescents aged 12-19**: 41 were using a low-dose pill (ethinyl estradiol 20 µg with desogestrel 150 µg), 26 were not; all had bone densitometry at baseline and after 12 months. At the **lumbar spine**, the median change in bone density was **+2.07%** in users against **+12.16%** in the others; for bone mineral content, +1.57% against +16.84%. For total body, the two curves were similar (Biason et al., 2015).

**How to read this study, so as not to turn it into an alarm:** it is small, **non-randomised** (those on the pill had chosen it, and users may differ for many other reasons), it lasts one year and it reports median changes. It does not establish causation, and it says nothing about fracture risk.

What the review dedicated to the topic adds, and which is more useful than any single study:

- the **ethinyl estradiol dose matters**: **20 µg** preparations may be insufficient for growing bone, those with **30 µg or more** appear more protective, and for an adolescent choosing the pill they are to be preferred;
- reversibility is **not demonstrated**: girls who had stopped the pill continued to have smaller gains in spine bone density than non-users even at 12 and 24 months after stopping;
- the usual things that work still apply to everyone: **adequate calcium and vitamin D**, weight-bearing activity, not smoking (Bachrach, 2020).

None of these lines is a reason to stop taking a contraceptive, and none replaces an assessment. They are the information needed to ask **one question** in the consulting room: for this girl who trains six hours a week, is there a better option for bone?

## Is the pill the answer for period pain?

**It can help, but it is not the first step and the evidence is more fragile than its reputation.**

The Cochrane review dedicated to the combined pill for primary dysmenorrhoea includes 10 studies; in the comparison with placebo, across a total of **497 women** for the pain outcome, the Peto odds ratio for pain relief is **2.01** (95% CI 1.32-3.08), with **significant heterogeneity** (I²=64%); excluding studies with inadequate allocation concealment the effect rises to 2.99 (95% CI 1.76-5.07). Reported adverse effects are nausea, headache and weight gain. The authors conclude that the evidence for pain improvement is **"limited"** (Wong et al., 2009).

In sporting practice there is one finding that cools expectations further: in the sample of Danish elite athletes, hormonal contraceptive use **did not abolish dysmenorrhoea**, although it appeared to reduce emotional-type symptoms (Oxfeldt et al., 2020).

The ACOG document dedicated to dysmenorrhoea in **adolescents** puts things in order: **NSAIDs** are the first-line treatment, hormonal methods are a later option, and if there is no improvement within **3-6 months** secondary causes — endometriosis above all — and adherence should be investigated (ACOG, 2018a). On what actually works against period pain, including exercise, there is [an article entirely dedicated to it](/en/blog/dolori-mestruali-giovani-atlete).

## Can the pill be used to move a period away from race day?

**It is a common practice among adult athletes, and it remains a medical decision.** In the sample of **186 Danish elite athletes**, **57%** used a hormonal contraceptive (74% combined, 26% progestin-only) and **60%** of pill users reported having manipulated their cycle through continuous use around competitions (Oxfeldt et al., 2020).

Three things to say precisely:

1. **These are adult, elite athletes, in Denmark.** It is not data about Italian adolescents, and citing it does not make it so.
2. **No study** has measured the consequences of making this choice at 15, in the years when bone mass is built. Absence of data is not reassurance.
3. **Coaches are not part of this.** They should not suggest it, arrange it, or build it into the season plan. If the question arises — and it does, especially when an important competition falls on an awkward day — the place to decide is a clinic, with the family.

The opposite observation also holds, and it often gets lost: **cycle phase, in itself, is not a good reason to give up a competition.** The average effect of menstrual phase on performance is trivial across 78 studies in adult women, with low evidence quality ([cycle and performance](/en/blog/ciclo-e-performance)). What changes results, far more than physiology, is the [practical handling of pain, leaks and kit](/en/blog/gestire-ciclo-nello-sport).

## Does the pill reduce the risk of an ACL tear?

**Some data suggest so, the evidence is low quality, and it is not a reason to take it.**

The systematic review with meta-analysis on the relationship between the menstrual cycle, contraceptives and the anterior cruciate ligament includes **21 studies and 68,758 participants**, 7 of them on hormonal contraceptives and injury. The two largest and highest-quality studies suggest a **protective** effect, with a risk reduction of **"up to 20%"**; ligament laxity was greater in the ovulatory than the follicular phase. The authors nonetheless judge the **overall strength of evidence to be low** and name as priorities observational studies with hormonal assays and interventional trials (Herzberg et al., 2017).

Translated for anyone who has to decide something on Monday: **no club and no sports physician should treat hormonal contraception as an injury-prevention strategy.** What has reduced ACL tears in young female athletes, on far stronger evidence, are [neuromuscular prevention programmes](/en/blog/crociato-giovani-atlete) — and they happen on the pitch, not in a pharmacy.

## What should a coach know?

**Three things, and none of them is about the drug.**

1. **An absent period is not an advantage and it is not normal.** In the Danish sample, 51% of athletes not using hormonal contraceptives reported menstrual disturbances, with a higher share in endurance sports (69%), and amenorrhoea was associated with a higher cardiovascular training volume (Oxfeldt et al., 2020). How common it is does not make it harmless: it makes it systematic.
2. **A pill bleed is not a health indicator.** If an athlete is on the pill, her cycle is no longer a useful signal for whether she is eating enough: you have to look at other things — load, weight, repeated injuries, mood, performance. This is why the guidelines ask that the athlete be **cautioned** about that masking effect (Williams et al., 2025).
3. **Contraception is not a changing-room topic.** It is not advised, not asked about in a group, not commented on. A coach's job is to get amenorrhoea to a healthcare professional and to build an environment where saying it costs nothing — on how that conversation is built without awkwardness, there is [a dedicated article](/en/blog/parlare-di-ciclo-con-allenatore).

## When to see a professional

These are situations where assessment should not be postponed:

- **no period by age 15**, or no period three years after breast development began;
- **a cycle absent for three months or more**, or fewer than 6-8 cycles in a year, on the pill or off it;
- **no period after stopping** a hormonal contraceptive;
- **period pain** that every month prevents training or going to school, or that does not improve within 3-6 months of starting treatment (ACOG, 2018a);
- **stress fractures**, or a fracture from minimal trauma, in an athlete with an irregular or absent cycle;
- the question "which method is best for me, training as much as I do?": it is a legitimate question and it has a clinical, not a generic, answer.

## Where BAB fits

BAB does not prescribe, does not recommend contraceptive methods and does not replace a clinic. It does one thing that is almost always missing from this story: **it keeps the signal.**

Because the weak point in everything written above is that nobody knows **how long** a girl has been without a period, how many competitions she has played in pain, how many sessions she has skipped. That data exists nowhere — and when the pill enters the picture, the cycle stops existing as an indicator too. BAB gives the athlete a private place to record it and gives the club only **aggregated, anonymous signals**, never the individual record. So that when she gets to the doctor, she does not arrive with an impression: she arrives with a history.
