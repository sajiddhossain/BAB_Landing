It happens every September, as reliably as the fixture list. The girl turns up at the gym with a blocked nose and a thick voice, the coach looks at her face, the girl says "I'm fine", and in the next thirty seconds something gets decided that neither of them really has the tools to decide.

The question — **does she train or stay home?** — looks trivial. It is not, for one precise reason: the two wrong answers cost different things. Keeping an athlete home for an ordinary cold costs her a session. Letting her train with a fever exposes her, in a rare but real minority of cases, to a cardiac risk. These are not symmetrical mistakes, and a good rule of thumb has to account for that.

This article sets out the most widely used rule in sport — the **neck check** — saying up front where it comes from and what it is worth; then it puts beside it the numbers we have on how often young female athletes actually fall ill, what happens to performance, and the few symptoms that move the decision from "let's see" to "no". With one limitation declared at the top: **most of the available evidence is in adult athletes**, often elite ones, and this article says so every time it applies.

> **Key points**
> - The **neck check** says: symptoms **above the neck** only → light activity possible; symptoms **below the neck** (fever, chills, deep cough, widespread aches) → stop. It originates in a **1993 clinical column** and has **never been validated** by a controlled trial (Eichner, 1993).
> - With a **fever** there is no activity: **myocarditis** is a recognised cause of arrhythmia and sudden cardiac death in athletes, and "continuing training during infections" is listed among the risk factors (Halle et al., 2020).
> - In **320 young Norwegian athletes** of mean age 16.2, on average **43%** had an ongoing health problem, and **illnesses accounted for 30%** of the total burden (Moseid et al., 2018).
> - At **Rio 2016**, across **11,274 ADULT athletes**, women had **40% more illnesses** than men, and **47%** of illnesses affected the respiratory system (Soligard et al., 2017).
> - In a systematic review of **17 studies in 7,793 people aged 15-65**, **mild** respiratory infections left cardiorespiratory endurance **largely intact**; severe ones did not (Kaulback et al., 2023).
> - In **84 adult athletes** with respiratory illness, **excessive fatigue** was the symptom most associated with a **prolonged return** (Schwellnus et al., 2021).
> - Among **789 professional athletes** (mean age 25, **98.5% men**) cardiac-screened after COVID-19, **3.8%** had an abnormal test and **0.6%** inflammatory heart disease (Martinez et al., 2021).

## What is the neck check, and where does it actually come from?

It is the rule almost every coach has heard at least once, usually without knowing who wrote it:

- **above the neck** — runny nose, blocked nose, sneezing, a scratchy throat, watery eyes — training can be attempted, at **reduced intensity and duration**, stopping if symptoms worsen during the session;
- **below the neck** — fever, chills, widespread muscle aches, a deep or productive cough, chest tightness or pain, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea — **you stop**.

Its origin is a clinical column published in 1993 in a sports medicine journal, written by a physician answering the question of "what to tell patients" about infection and exercise (Eichner, 1993). From there it became common property in changing rooms and clinics worldwide.

It matters to be honest about what it is: **a piece of clinical common sense, not an experimental result**. No controlled trial has randomised athletes with a cold to train or to rest and measured what happens. The value of the rule is that it **shifts the conversation from the calendar to the symptoms**: not "when is the match?", but "where are the symptoms, and has there been a fever?". That is already a lot.

The International Olympic Committee consensus statement on acute respiratory illness in athletes (Schwellnus et al., 2022) is the modern reference on this ground: it proposes shared definitions and classifications, and is written for the sports physician deciding case by case. It does not replace the rule of thumb in the gym — it frames it.

## Why fever is the line that is not negotiable

Caution here is not a mannerism. The reason has a name: **myocarditis**, inflammation of the heart muscle that can follow a viral infection.

A clinical review devoted specifically to athletes describes it this way: myocarditis is **an important cause of arrhythmia and sudden cardiac death** in physically active people, and among the reasons elite athletes appear more exposed the review explicitly lists **continuing to train during infections or resuming too early**, alongside pathogen exposure from travel and extreme exertion (Halle et al., 2020). The symptoms it presents with are non-specific and very easy to mistake for "start-of-season tiredness": **fatigue, muscle soreness, a higher than usual heart rate at rest and during exercise, and reduced exercise capacity**.

How rare is it? An order of magnitude comes from the systematic cardiac screening run by the North American professional leagues after COVID-19: across **789 professional athletes** (mean age 25, **98.5% men**), **3.8%** had at least one abnormal first-line test and ultimately **5 athletes, 0.6%**, had cardiac magnetic resonance findings suggesting inflammatory heart disease (Martinez et al., 2021). That population is a long way from a fifteen-year-old — adults, almost all male, professional, after one specific infection — and it serves only to say two things at once: **it is rare**, and **it is not zero**.

Hence the rule that holds at every age: **with a fever there is no physical activity**. Not light activity, not "just the warm-up", not for the important match. And after a fever, the return starts slowly.

## How often do young female athletes really fall ill?

This is the number missing from almost every conversation in the gym, where illness is treated as an inconvenience and injury as a serious problem.

A Norwegian prospective study followed **320 young athletes** for **26 weeks** — 260 at elite sport academy high schools and 60 of their classmates at ordinary schools, mean age **16.2**, boys and girls — asking them each week to report injuries and illnesses through a validated questionnaire. The result: at any given time, on average **43%** of the elite sport athletes had **an ongoing health problem** (95% confidence interval 37-49%), and **25%** a *substantial* problem, meaning one that reduced participation or performance. And above all: the total burden split almost into thirds between **overuse injuries (37%)**, **acute injuries (34%)** and **illnesses (30%)** (Moseid et al., 2018).

Illnesses were distributed differently across sports: more common in **endurance** sports (23%) than in **technical** (10%) and **team** sports (8%). It is a study of boys and girls together, in an elite school setting: it does not describe the volleyball player at a small local club. But it dismantles a widespread idea — that illness is background noise. **It carries about a third of the problem.**

On the adult side there is a far larger and far more specific figure. In the prospective surveillance of the **Rio 2016 Olympic Games**, across **11,274 athletes** (45% women), **5.4 illnesses per 100 athletes** were recorded over 17 days; **5%** of athletes had at least one illness, **47%** of illnesses affected **the respiratory system**, and **women had 40% more illnesses than men** (Soligard et al., 2017). That 40% is a robust figure — but in **adult Olympic athletes**, during an event with intercontinental travel, shared accommodation and extreme competition loads. It should be cited for what it is: a signal worth studying in adolescents, not a number to transfer to them.

## Does a cold hurt performance? Less than feared

Here comes the counterintuitive part, and it is worth underlining because it is what makes the decision calmer: **if the infection is mild, the body copes better than people think**.

The IOC consensus subgroup ran a **systematic review** on exactly this point: 17 studies, **7,793 people**, aged **15 to 65**. The conclusions, with all the caution that substantial methodological heterogeneity demands: **cardiorespiratory endurance** was **largely unaffected** by mild respiratory infections (seven studies); **pulmonary function tests** fall measurably but stay **within normal ranges**; **more severe** infections, however, are associated with a negative impact on training and performance, and longer-term effects on performance were divided across studies (Kaulback et al., 2023).

What does that give you, practically? That with an ordinary cold **the reason to stop is not performance**. It is the risk of turning something small into something long — and the fact that training badly, reluctantly and with your head elsewhere does not train much. One light session done well is worth more than a normal session done at half measure.

## Does heavy training lower the defences?

For twenty years the automatic answer was yes, on the strength of the so-called **open window**: in the hours after hard exertion, lymphocytes in the blood collapse, so — the argument went — the defences drop and viruses get in.

A widely cited review took that chain of reasoning apart piece by piece: the evidence that vigorous exercise increases opportunistic infection is, it argues, limited; changes in salivary immunoglobulins do not signpost suppression; and above all the fall in lymphocytes **1-2 hours after** exercise is a **temporary redistribution** of cells towards peripheral tissues — that is, **more immune surveillance**, not less. The same review presents evidence that frequent physical activity **improves** immune competency, including the response to vaccines (Campbell and Turner, 2018). It is a narrative review and an avowedly partisan position in the debate: it should be read as such, not as a final verdict.

What does hold is a different and solid piece: **load management**. The International Olympic Committee consensus statement on load in sport concludes that **inappropriate load management** — rapid increases, a congested calendar, psychological load, travel — is a significant risk factor for acute illness and overtraining, and proposes practical guidance for monitoring load and athlete wellbeing (Schwellnus et al., 2016). It is a consensus on elite athletes, largely adult.

Translated for a fifteen-year-old: **it is not training that makes her ill**. It is the increase that comes too fast, the week with three matches and two exams, the six-hour nights. That can be acted on — and it is exactly the ground that the articles on [sleep in teenage athletes](/en/blog/sonno-atlete-adolescenti) and on [low energy availability](/en/blog/red-s-bassa-disponibilita-energetica) cross from two other directions.

## How to come back, step by step

There is no universal protocol, and anyone proposing a precise day-by-day one is going beyond the data. There is, however, a cautious structure, modelled on the way athletes return after other stoppages, and its main job is to **stop you skipping the next-day check**:

1. **Prerequisite**: no fever for at least **24-48 hours** and **no fever-reducing medicines**. If there was a high fever or a significant illness, a doctor authorises the restart.
2. **Days 1-2**: very light activity — walking, flat cycling, mobility — for 20-30 minutes. The goal is not to train: it is to see how the body responds.
3. **Days 3-4**: light to moderate aerobic work, with the team but outside the intense drills.
4. **Then**: intensity and volume rise one step at a time, **one a day**, and you only climb if the following day brings no worsening.
5. **Last**: maximal efforts, contact, match play.

One rule holds the whole ladder together: **you judge the day after, not during**. If marked fatigue, a low fever or a high resting heart rate come back after the first session, you drop a step and wait.

And a list of **signs to stop and see a doctor**, because they are the same ones myocarditis presents with in athletes (Halle et al., 2020): **chest pain or tightness**, **breathlessness out of proportion to the effort**, a **raised resting heart rate** that does not settle, **fainting or near-fainting**, **fatigue that worsens instead of improving**.

If the tiredness lasts weeks, it is worth ruling other things out: [glandular fever](/en/blog/mononucleosi-sport-giovani-atlete) can present in an athlete with nothing more than a drop in energy, and [iron deficiency](/en/blog/ferro-atlete-adolescenti) also looks a lot like "she does not recover any more".

## And if the breathlessness never goes away?

One case deserves a parenthesis, because it gets mistaken for "a cold that will not heal" every autumn: the girl who, after every infection, is left for weeks with a cough and breathlessness on exertion. Sometimes it is not the infection failing to clear — it is **exercise-induced bronchoconstriction** that the infection has made visible. It has its own diagnostic criteria and its own pathway, which [the article on breathlessness](/en/blog/fiato-corto-giovani-atlete) covers at length: here it is enough to know that "she tires more than the others after every cold" is a sentence to take to a doctor, not to file away.

## What a coach can do (and cannot)

**You can:**

- **ask two questions instead of one**: "where are the symptoms?" and "have you had a fever in the last 48 hours?". Those are the two pieces of information that change the decision;
- **make it explicit that stopping does not cost a place**: if stopping carries a sporting price, girls learn to hide symptoms, and the first to lose out is the coach;
- **offer an alternative rather than an absence**: being at the gym without training, mobility work, light technical work. It strips illness of its meaning as exclusion;
- **plan a graded return** and judge **the day after**, not during the session;
- **remember that bottles, cups and towels are not shared**: it is the most banal measure and the most effective one in a squad;
- **take to the sports physician** — including at [the eligibility exam](/en/blog/visita-idoneita-sportiva-giovani-atlete) — any story of long-lasting fatigue, persistent breathlessness or an odd heart rate after an illness.

**You cannot:**

- play someone with a **fever**, in any circumstance and for any match;
- use fever-reducing medicines to "cover" symptoms and get her onto the pitch: masking a fever does not remove the risk, it hides it;
- read prolonged tiredness as **a lack of commitment**;
- decide the return by looking at the calendar instead of the girl.

The September cold, in almost every case, is exactly what it looks like: a four-day nuisance. The job of the adults around the athlete is not to turn it into a drama — it is to hold **one single line**, the fever line, and to make sure that saying so costs her nothing.
